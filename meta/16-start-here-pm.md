# Start Here — Product Managers
**Type:** Audience orientation meta-piece | **Not published** — referenced from library introduction and capability calibration guide

## The problems this reading path addresses

Product Managers in this context commonly encounter: requirements that don't have a named problem behind them; feature requests that describe a solution without the user situation; user validation that looks like stakeholder approval; post-delivery surprise about how users actually engage; and a sense that design involvement means added process rather than clearer decisions.

The pieces in this path address those directly — without naming them as "PM problems." Every piece delivers something usable immediately.

## What PM audiences often misunderstand about design's role

From primary research with 16+ PMs across the org: common perception gaps include "design can't help with SaaS or third-party software," "design is only UI polish, not substantive problem-solving," "design doesn't use data-driven approaches," and "design can't help solve complex business problems." These assumptions shape what PMs ask design for — and what they don't. This library quietly addresses each through the substance of what it teaches, not by naming or arguing the perception.

## Recommended starter sequence (5 pieces)

| Order | Piece | Why this one first |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 122 — Starting Questions | The pre-work ritual before any project starts; converts vague asks into checkable claims |
| 2 | 125 — Jobs-to-Be-Done | Reframes requirements from solutions to user situations; most directly addresses the "describe what to build" gap |
| 3 | 107 — Framing the Problem | Turns a JTBD situation into a crisp problem statement; moves from "what we know" to "what we're solving" |
| 4 | 113 — Defining Success Before You Start | Converts a problem statement into a measurable outcome; prevents post-delivery interpretation of results |
| 5 | 207 — Premortem | Pre-tests the plan against likely failure modes before resources are committed |

After these five, a PM can write a brief that names the job being done, the expected outcome, and the risks — before development starts.

## Tier context for this audience

Most PM work begins at **Tier 100 — Recognize**: developing the ability to name what kind of problem you're in, before deciding how to solve it. Tier 200 becomes relevant when the PM is running specific research or facilitation themselves; Tier 300 when they're orchestrating a multi-step design-informed workflow.

## Audience-specific T300 arcs

Eight arcs deliver systems-level judgment specifically for PM contexts. Three are core; five extend into specialized PM challenges:

**Core PM arcs — start with these:**

| Arc | What it delivers |
|---|---|
| 311 — Psychology of Stakeholder Decisions | Diagnose which cognitive mechanism (Reactance, Loss Aversion, Genuine Concern) is driving stakeholder resistance, and choose a response matched to the mechanism |
| 314 — How People Actually Decide | Design decision contexts — not just decision options — to support the quality of deliberation consequential decisions deserve |
| 316 — Reading Data Without Getting Fooled | Evaluate analytics findings at a skeptical practitioner level: identify what a metric actually proves, who's missing from the data, and what the test result doesn't claim |

311 builds most directly on the starter sequence above. 314 and 316 can be read in any order after the starter. Prereqs for 311: 247 (Dual Process Theory), 248 (Framing Effect), 249 (Loss Aversion) — all T100 and readable in under 5 minutes each.

**Extended PM arcs — for the full PM practice:**

| Arc | What it delivers |
|---|---|
| 317 — From a Vague Ask to a Solvable Problem | Convert incoming asks into testable problem statements before committing to a solution direction |
| 318 — Writing Requirements That Survive the Build | Write specs that answer the four questions a developer will face, surface hidden UX decisions before implementation, and survive contact with the build |
| 319 — Lightweight Validation Without a Research Team | Run a concept test, impression test, or one-task session yourself — fast enough to get signal before a decision point |
| 320 — Prioritizing Without False Precision | Use RICE/ICE frameworks honestly: name the assumptions behind the inputs, make uncertainty explicit, and communicate reprioritization decisions credibly |
| 321 — Managing the Design Feedback Loop | Run design reviews that produce decisions — define the right session type, classify feedback in real time, close on named decisions, and protect approved work from re-litigation |

317 and 318 form a natural pair (problem definition → requirements). 319 can be read independently; highest cross-audience value. 320 and 321 address late-stage PM judgment challenges.
