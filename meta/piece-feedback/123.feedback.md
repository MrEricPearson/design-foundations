## Reviewer 2 — Dr. Magdalena Dybas-Stronkowska (Doctorate, Behavioral Research)

**Form:** Library Review Feedback ID 11 (2026-09-10)

**Ratings:** Clarity 4/5 · Opportunity for improvement 5/5 · Ready to publish: Yes, with minor edits

**What worked:**
- "Great piece overall. Easy to read, accessible to a non-UX audience, and does a great job of packing a lot of important concepts into a relatively short article."

**What didn't work / suggestions:**
1. After the "that wasn't usability testing / the distinction matters" beat, add a one-line takeaway readers should keep: the goal is not to learn what users think about the product — it's to discover whether they can successfully use it on their own.
2. Emphasize opinions vs. behavior more strongly: interviews, surveys, demos, and feedback sessions tell you what people think, believe, remember, or prefer. Usability testing shows what they actually do.
3. Name that people cannot always accurately describe their own behavior. They're usually honest; they still don't always remember, notice, or describe what they did. Observation catches issues nobody would mention in an interview or survey.
4. After "you're not helping them do it," say why: that's what real use looks like — no PM, developer, or designer sitting next to them to explain the product or recover from mistakes.
5. Brief recap of the insight types the method is built to catch: confusion, hesitation, mistakes, incorrect assumptions, task failures.
6. The line "Feedback happens before you've built much" oversimplifies. Feedback can land on shipped features; usability testing can run on sketches. Prefer: feedback is reactions, opinions, and preferences; usability testing is task completion and observed behavior.
7. The search-bar / click-data parenthetical is analytics, not usability testing. Swap for a testing-shaped instance: everyone says the form is easy; three of five people in the session can't find Submit. Both answers are honest. Only one reveals the usability problem.
