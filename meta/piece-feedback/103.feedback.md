## Reviewer 1 — Carolina Louro (UX Strategist)

**What worked:**
- Second-person voice throughout ("you," "try") — feels like someone talking directly to me, not just content
- The article gives the message: don't fall in love with what you're doing, be open to feedback and challenge assumptions
- Gives next steps — makes the reader want to read the next chapter right away
- IKEA effect paragraph: tangible, immediately understandable, easy to connect with
- Ross/Lepper and Kahneman paragraphs worked because they spoke to the reader directly — the citation was in service of the conversation, not a stand-alone academic reference
- Formatting and images will help it land even better

**What didn't work:**
- The Barry Staw (1976) paragraph was hard to connect with. Felt academic in its presentation — the investing/stock market framing was harder to relate to than the other examples. Made her want to skip that section more than any other.
- The issue wasn't the content (she understood it) — it was the flow. The Staw paragraph reads like quoting a study rather than talking to the reader.
- Being a non-native English speaker may have amplified this, but the structural issue is real regardless of language background.

**Key insight:** What makes citations land in this piece isn't the researcher's name — it's whether the paragraph is still talking TO the reader. Ross/Lepper and Kahneman worked because they stayed in the second-person conversation. Staw broke the flow by centering on the study's setup (investing, 1976) rather than the reader's experience.

**Proposed revision:** The Staw paragraph needs to either be replaced with a simpler, more tangible example, or rewritten to stay in conversation with the reader rather than describing the research setup. The IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon, Ariely) is the model to emulate.

## Reviewer 2 — Dr. Magdalena Dybas-Stronkowska (Doctorate, Behavioral Research)

**What worked:**
- Using behavioral economics and psychology to explain natural tendencies — interesting and intellectually grounded
- Sections that speak directly to the reader (inviting them to run a test, observe their own reaction) are the most engaging
- Format: short, clearly defined sections, easy to scan

**What didn't work:**

**Audience specificity:** Intended audience wasn't immediately clear. The "building" and "designing" framing is generic — more specific references to the actual work context (creating products for planners, farmers, logistics teams) would make it more immediately recognizable.

**Consequences underexplicit:** What actually happens when a team becomes too attached to assumptions and less open to challenge? The downstream impact on users, product outcomes, and business is implied but not stated.

**Barry Staw citation:** Same issue as Reviewer 1. The study is too complex to summarize in a few sentences without losing clarity. The investing context requires too much setup to land quickly. Recommends replacing with a simpler, more intuitive example, or moving to footnotes/references only.

**Researcher name handling:** Names alone don't add value to readers unfamiliar with the field. Either contextualize briefly ("Daniel Kahneman, a Nobel Prize-winning economist") or use discipline framing ("behavioral economics research shows..."). In many cases the discipline attribution is cleaner.

**Research-vs-extrapolation:** The "Here's a simple test worth running" section draws a direct connection between research findings and team behavior that may be the author's extrapolation rather than a finding from the cited sources. Two concerns: (1) it implicitly divides teams into good/bad; (2) team reactions to feedback have many legitimate causes (fear of leadership, dynamics, business pressure) that aren't named. Either acknowledge the complexity or narrow the frame to what the research actually supports.

## Synthesis — Revision Priorities

**Must fix before re-review:**
1. Barry Staw paragraph — rewrite or replace. The investing framing breaks the conversational flow. Two reviewers independently flagged this section. Either find a simpler substitute or rewrite to stay in second-person conversation rather than describing the study setup.
2. Researcher names — add one-phrase context for any named researcher the practitioner audience wouldn't recognize, or switch to discipline attribution.
3. Research-vs-extrapolation clarity in the "simple test" section — narrow the claim or acknowledge team-reaction complexity.

**Worth addressing in revision:**
4. Consequences — add a sentence naming the downstream impact on users, product, or business when attachment takes hold.
5. Audience specificity — consider making at least one reference more specific to the actual work context.

**Leave alone:**
- Second-person voice, direct address — explicitly praised by both reviewers, confirmed as a strength
- IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon, Ariely) — praised as tangible and immediately understandable; is the model for the Staw fix
- Overall structure and forward momentum — working well
