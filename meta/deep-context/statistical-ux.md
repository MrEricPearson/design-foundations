# Deep Context: Statistical UX
**Type:** Deep Context awareness piece | **Not a method piece** — no steps, no how-to | **Not published as standalone content**

**What it is:** Statistical UX (also called quantitative UX research) uses inferential statistics to draw conclusions from UX data — determining whether a finding from a sample reflects real behavior in a larger population, whether a design change produced a measurable difference, and how confident we can be in a result before acting on it.

**What it reveals that simpler methods can't:** Qualitative methods (interviews, usability sessions) tell you what's happening and why; statistical methods tell you how reliably it's happening across your full user population. A usability session where 4 of 5 users struggled with a task is directionally informative. A statistically valid study might reveal whether that's 80% of all users or 20% — a difference that determines whether the problem is critical or marginal. Statistical UX also enables A/B test result interpretation, sample size calculations, and regression analysis of UX metrics against business outcomes.

**What you'll need to run it:** Statistical literacy (understanding p-values, confidence intervals, effect sizes, and sample size requirements); tools for analysis (R, Python, or dedicated survey analysis platforms); and sample sizes that vary from 100–1,000+ depending on the effect size being measured. Most meaningful statistical UX work requires a Researcher with quantitative methods training.

**What atoms in this library give you the concept:** 135 (Leading vs. Lagging Indicators) and 217 (UX Metrics) give you the foundation for defining what to measure. 146 (What Sample Size Means — forthcoming) will give the intuition for when you need statistical evidence vs. when directional qualitative data is sufficient.

**When to bring it up with a specialist:** when a design decision will affect a large user population and the directional signal from qualitative work isn't sufficient; when an A/B test result needs to be interpreted as significant or not; or when UX metrics need to be connected to business outcomes with statistical confidence. Bring it to a Researcher or data scientist — the tools and training are specialist territory.
