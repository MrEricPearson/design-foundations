# Deep Context: Biometric UX Research
**Type:** Deep Context awareness piece | **Not a method piece** — no steps, no how-to | **Not published as standalone content**

**What it is:** Biometric UX research uses physiological data to measure emotional and cognitive responses to design — including galvanic skin response (GSR/EDA, measuring arousal), facial coding (analyzing micro-expressions for emotional valence), heart rate variability (cognitive load), and EEG (brain activity patterns). These methods capture involuntary responses that self-report can't access.

**What it reveals that simpler methods can't:** Conscious self-report has well-documented limitations: users can't accurately recall emotional states, are subject to social desirability bias, and often rationalize post-hoc what was actually an emotional or automatic response. Biometric methods access pre-conscious and involuntary reactions — a user might say an interface feels "fine" while their GSR shows elevated arousal (stress or excitement) at a specific interaction point. This is especially valuable for high-stakes interfaces (healthcare, financial decisions, emergency contexts) where emotional response affects behavior.

**What you'll need to run it:** Specialized hardware (GSR wristbands, facial coding software, EEG headsets); specialized analysis software and expertise; controlled lab or remote conditions; and researchers trained in psychophysiology. Biometric research is typically a lab study requirement. Consumer-grade products exist (Tobii, iMotions) but analysis still requires specialist interpretation.

**What atoms in this library give you the concept:** 205 (Content Design / UX Writing) addresses emotional tone in content, which biometrics can measure empirically. The desire-paths standalone piece addresses behavioral signal — biometrics extends that to physiological signal from designed stimuli.

**When to bring it up with a specialist:** when you're designing for emotionally high-stakes contexts and need to validate whether the design creates stress, trust, or confidence — especially when self-report is unreliable in the domain (e.g., users underreporting anxiety in medical interfaces). This is specialist research and most enterprise design contexts won't require it; it's worth knowing it exists for when it matters.
