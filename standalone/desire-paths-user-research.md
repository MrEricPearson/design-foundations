# Desire Paths as Literal User Research
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to recognize where people are already deviating from a designed flow — and name those deviations as research data rather than user error.

**Trigger:** you're not sure your designed flow matches what people actually do.

**Method:** look at where people deviate from the intended path — a shortcut, a workaround, a feature used in an unintended way. That deviation is data.

**Artifact:** a short list of "unintended paths" worth investigating.

**Proof:** Unintended paths exist whether or not you've listed them — they're already happening. Writing them down doesn't create them; it makes what users are actually doing visible enough to decide, on purpose, whether to design around it.

**Don't confuse a desire path with a bug** — a bug is something failing to work as intended; a desire path is something working as intended while users route around it anyway. Bugs are design failures; desire paths are evidence that the intended design doesn't match the actual need. Both need addressing, but only one tells you something about user intent.

**Try This:** look at something you've shipped. Ask one person who uses it how they actually do [the main task]. Watch without explaining the "right" way. Note anywhere their path differs from yours. That's a desire path.

**What Next:** if a desire path reveals a meaningful gap between intended and actual use, read 206c (Assumption-First Proto-Map) to map the real flow alongside the designed one — you likely don't need observations first, just what your team already knows. If the workaround appears repeatedly across people, it's a research signal worth investigating: read 202a (Conversational Interview) if you can get 20 minutes with someone, or 202b (Remote/Async Research) if you need asynchronous input.
