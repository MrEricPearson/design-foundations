# 206a — Discovery-Driven Journey Map
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Journey Mapping (see 206-journey-mapping-arc.md for approach selection) | **Prereqs:** 175 (What a Journey Map Is), 153 (Experience vs. Process), 154 (What a Pain Point Is), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 202 (Research Mechanics — for generating the evidence this piece draws from) | **Note:** Use when you have research findings or observations. Prereq for 306 (Service Blueprint).

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to build a journey map from research findings and observations — grounded in what users actually do, with evidence cited at each step.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of a finding from research — an interview, a support ticket, something you observed — where what users actually did surprised your team. That moment of surprise is the raw material a discovery-driven map is built from. A map built this way is different from every other version because the surprise is still visible in it: the steps users actually take, not the steps you expected them to take.

**Trigger:** You have observations, interview notes, behavioral data, or research findings — and you need to turn that evidence into a map your team can act on.

**Why this works:** Evidence-cited maps change how teams handle disagreement. When the source of each step is visible — what research produced it, how many sources support it — teams argue less about whether the map is real and argue more about what to do about it. The citation isn't formality; it's the mechanism for converting findings into decisions.

**Method:**
1. **Gather your evidence.** Pull together what you have: interview notes, observation records, support tickets, analytics exports, session recordings. Mixed sources and mixed quality are fine — you just need to know which source supports which claim.
2. **Scope the map.** Name the persona and the goal. The persona definition should be grounded in your evidence, not your team's prior belief — if your research covered different user types, scope this map to the one with the strongest evidence base first. Map one persona, one goal, per document.
3. **Extract steps from evidence, not from expectation.** For each step, the source should be a specific observation or finding. If you find yourself writing a step because "that's how the process is supposed to work," you've crossed from evidence to assumption — label it as an assumption and mark it explicitly.
4. **Mark evidence quality.** For each step, note what the evidence is and roughly how many sources support it. "Observed: 4 participants" carries different weight than "Heard: one interview." This doesn't need elaborate notation — a simple count or source label per step is enough to make the difference visible.
5. **Mark pain points from evidence.** Pain points should cite the specific observations that revealed them. "3 participants stopped here." "Every person we observed had to re-enter this information." Inferred pain points are allowed — label them as hypothesized.

**Artifact:** An evidence-cited journey map — steps labeled with source notes, pain points with finding citations, and any inferred steps explicitly marked.

**Watchout:** Evidence-cited maps become stale faster than proto-maps. Users and processes change; a map from 18 months ago may describe a process that no longer exists. Put a date on the map and note the research it draws from. A map with no date and no source reference will eventually be treated as current fact regardless of when it was made — and the team will defend it as vigorously as if it were.

**Try This:** Take research findings you already have — even a handful of interviews or a few support tickets. Extract the steps a user goes through from those findings. For each step, note where you got the information (even "support ticket, 2 mentions"). Mark the worst pain point you can cite evidence for. That is a discovery-driven map.

**Proof:** For every step on the map, you can answer: "What's the evidence for this?" If any step can only be answered with "we assumed it," label it or remove it. A discovery-driven map where every sourced step has a source note and every assumed step is labeled as such is qualitatively different from any other version of this document — the distinction is visible in the map, not just in your head.

**Take this further:** In the next week, share the map with someone who wasn't in the research. Ask them to challenge one step: "What's the evidence for this?" If the challenge reveals a step that's actually team assumption that crept in — update the map. Write one sentence: which assumption slipped into the evidence-based map despite the sourcing effort?

**After you've run this yourself:** Describe your research findings (themes, observations, key patterns) to an AI tool and ask it to draft a journey map sequence from them. Compare the AI draft to what you built. Where it contains steps you don't have evidence for, that's the over-inference problem — name it explicitly. Where it missed steps you do have evidence for, that's the signal about what the evidence makes visible that interpretation alone misses.

**What Next:** If the map reveals gaps your current research doesn't cover, plan to fill them — see 202 (Research Mechanics) for approach options. If you need to compare this persona's experience to another role's, add swimlanes (176: Swimlanes as a Comparison Tool). If pain points cluster around a handoff between teams, read 303 (One Feature, Three Handoffs). When ready to map the systems and teams behind each step, read 306 (Service Blueprint).
