# 301c — Assumption-First Proto-Persona
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Companion to 301 (From Vague Ask to Real Persona) | **Prereqs:** 173 (Empathy as Cognitive Simulation), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude) | **Note:** Use when research access is unavailable and you need a working persona now. When research becomes available, take the proto-persona into the 301 arc.

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to build a structured, labeled proto-persona from team knowledge — so you have something you can make design decisions with now, rather than waiting for research access you may not have.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of a product decision that was made about a user type your team hadn't actually spoken to yet. The decision was made anyway — because it had to be. The persona in those decisions is almost always implicit and unlabeled, which means it's also unchallengeable. A labeled proto-persona makes that same mental model explicit, visible, and updatable.

**Trigger:** You need to make design decisions about a user you don't have direct research access to — client employees behind a firewall, users in a regulated industry, a user type your team hasn't engaged yet. You need a working model now.

**Why this works:** The proto-persona doesn't create knowledge you don't have. It makes explicit the model you're already using — because you're making design decisions for someone, and whatever those decisions assume about that person is your current working model, labeled or not. Labeling it makes the gaps visible; visible gaps can be investigated. Unlabeled assumptions can't.

**Method:**
1. **Name the role and primary goal.** Write: "[Role] trying to [primary goal]." One role, one goal. If you're designing for multiple roles, each gets its own proto-persona document.
2. **Fill in what your team knows.** Work through four fields:
   - **Context:** What environment do they work in? What tools, processes, or constraints shape their day? What do they know coming in that your product assumes or depends on?
   - **Goals:** What are they trying to accomplish — not just in your product, but in the broader task your product serves? What does success look like from their perspective?
   - **Frustrations:** What currently makes this hard? Where does the existing process break down for them?
   - **Behaviors:** What do they actually do? (Not what they should do — what you've observed or heard they do.)
3. **Mark confidence.** For each field, note how you know what you wrote: "Seen" (direct observation), "Heard" (told by someone who's talked to them), or "Assuming" (team inference with no external data). Use any consistent marking.
4. **Name what you don't know.** Write one sentence per field about the question that, if answered, would most change what you put there. These become your research priorities when access opens up.

**Artifact:** A structured proto-persona — four labeled fields, with confidence markers on each entry, and a list of open questions that would upgrade it.

**Watchout:** A proto-persona without confidence markers is not a useful persona — it's a set of team assumptions formatted to look like research. The markers are what prevent the proto-persona from being treated as validated. If someone reads the proto-persona and assumes the "Behaviors" field reflects what users actually do, the markers haven't been visible enough. Put them in a place that's impossible to miss.

**When You Can't Run the Full Version**

**If you can't gather even informal team knowledge:** Start with one field only — Goals. Ask: "What is this person trying to accomplish?" That one field, labeled as a team assumption, is the starting point for every design decision. It is infinitely more useful than an implicit, unlabeled assumption.

**What you still get:** One explicit, challengeable assumption instead of one implicit, unchallengeable one.

**What you give up:** The cross-field coherence that makes a full proto-persona useful — the relationship between context, goals, frustrations, and behaviors is where the persona becomes useful for decision-making.

**Don't do this:** Don't fill in confidence markers retroactively after the persona has been shared and used. The markers need to be visible in the version people use to make decisions — not added later. A proto-persona used without markers is a proto-persona used as if it were validated.

**Try This:** Pick the user type you're currently designing for most. Fill in the four fields using what your team knows today. Mark each entry with "Seen," "Heard," or "Assuming." Write one open question per field. That is your proto-persona.

**Proof:** Count the "Assuming" entries. If the proto-persona has many "Assuming" entries, you now know specifically what to investigate when research access opens. If it has many "Seen" entries, you may have more direct access than you thought — those "Seen" entries can feed directly into the 301 arc. Either count is useful information about where your design decisions currently rest.

**Take this further:** In the next 2-3 days, share the proto-persona with one person on your team who has had the most contact with this user type — a support rep, an account manager, a PM who's taken calls with them. Ask: "What did we get wrong or miss?" Update the confidence markers where their knowledge changes what you wrote. Write one sentence: what field changed most based on what they told you?

**After you've run this yourself:** Paste the proto-persona into an AI tool and ask: "What assumptions in this persona are most likely to be wrong based on common patterns for this type of user in this context?" Use the output as a checklist of things to verify when research access becomes available — not as corrections.

**What Next:** When you have access to the actual users this persona represents — even for one short conversation — take the proto-persona into the 301 arc (From Vague Ask to Real Persona) to add evidence to its fields. The arc is designed to move a proto-persona from assumed to validated, starting from exactly what you'll have built here.

If the proto-persona reveals that you're designing for multiple distinct roles rather than one, each role that differs meaningfully in context, goals, frustrations, or behaviors gets its own proto-persona. Map the differences before building the same product for all of them.
