# 206b — Co-Creative Journey Map
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Journey Mapping (see 206-journey-mapping-arc.md for approach selection) | **Prereqs:** 175 (What a Journey Map Is), 153 (Experience vs. Process), 154 (What a Pain Point Is), 163 (Alignment vs. Consensus), 164 (Generative vs. Convergent Thinking), 304 (Running One Workshop — for general facilitation mechanics) | **Note:** Use when alignment and shared ownership are part of the goal alongside documentation. Prereq for 306 (Service Blueprint).

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to facilitate a session that produces a shared journey map — where the act of building it together generates as much value as the artifact itself.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of a time when your team disagreed about what users go through — and each person was confident in their version. The disagreement wasn't about what to do; it was about what's actually happening. A co-creative map resolves that kind of deadlock by making the building of the shared model a collective act — so the map belongs to the room, not to design.

**Trigger:** Alignment and shared ownership are part of the goal alongside documentation — you need the people in the room to feel invested in the map. Or: you have access to users directly in a session, and capturing their experience in their own words is more valuable than your team's interpretation of it.

**Why this works:** People trust artifacts they helped build more than artifacts handed to them. When the room builds the map, it carries both the collective knowledge and the collective investment in acting on it. The facilitation structure is what separates productive building from group sensemaking that goes in circles.

**Method:**
1. **Establish scope before the session.** Decide the persona and goal in advance — don't open the session by negotiating scope. Scope negotiation in the room consumes facilitation time and produces compromise scopes that help no one. Put the persona and goal on the board before anyone arrives.
2. **Seed the map.** Open with a skeleton, not a blank board. Add 3-5 rough steps you believe are directionally true, marked as seeds, not answers. This gives participants something to respond to rather than blank space to fill from scratch. Blank space produces silence; seeds produce corrections and additions — which is what you want.
3. **Invite additions and corrections.** Ask the room to add steps they know, correct steps that are wrong, and mark where they've personally seen friction. Give participants a way to contribute independently first (sticky notes, individual writing time), then share. Independent generation before group discussion prevents the loudest voice from setting the frame.
4. **Surface divergence — don't resolve it.** When participants disagree about a step — whether it exists, what it looks like, whether it's a pain point — mark the divergence on the map rather than settling it in the moment. A visible "two different pictures of this step" is itself a finding. Resolving it in the room produces premature convergence; marking it preserves the information.
5. **Label the map honestly at close.** The map is a collective mental model — say so explicitly at the end of the session. If the participants included actual users, note it and note how many. If the participants were all internal, say that — it changes what the map represents and how it should be treated in subsequent decisions.

**Artifact:** A shared-ownership journey map — built collectively, labeled as a mental model with participant source noted, with divergences marked rather than resolved.

**Watchout:** A room that shares the same blindspot produces a polished map of that blindspot. If everyone in the room is from the same team, the map reflects that team's understanding — not necessarily the user's reality. Co-creation captures what the room believes; producing a validated map requires either users in the room or a post-session validation step with actual users.

**When You Can't Run the Full Version**

**If you can't gather participants synchronously:** Run the map-building in asynchronous stages. Stage 1: share the persona and goal, and ask each participant to write their version of the steps independently (a simple numbered list is enough). Stage 2: compile responses and surface the divergences — where people named different steps or different pain points. Stage 3: run a short synchronous session only to resolve the divergences, not to build the whole map. The async approach takes longer but often produces more honest input because participants reflect before responding.

**What you still get:** The alignment value — participants have engaged with the question individually, and the map carries their collective perspective.

**What you give up:** The generative energy of live building, where one person's step sparks a correction or addition from someone else in real time. Async maps tend to be more individually complete but less cross-pollinated.

**Don't do this:** Don't skip the individual generation stage and go straight to a shared document where participants can see each other's answers before writing their own. Group visibility before individual thought produces anchoring — the first entry shapes all subsequent ones.

**Try This:** For a team or product area where you know there's disagreement about what users experience: gather the relevant people (or run the async version above), put the persona and goal at the top, and seed the map with 3-5 rough steps. Let the room add, correct, and mark friction. At close, label what you have: a collective mental model, with participant list and date noted.

**Proof:** The map has divergences marked — places where participants disagreed about a step or a pain point. If the map has no marked divergences, either the room had perfect alignment (unlikely) or the facilitation smoothed over disagreement rather than surfacing it. Marked divergence is a success indicator for this method — it means the facilitation captured what was actually in the room.

**Take this further:** In the next 3-5 days, validate one section of the co-created map with a single person who goes through this experience but wasn't in the room. Ask only: "What did we get wrong?" Write one sentence about what the collective map assumed that didn't hold for someone outside it.

**After you've run this yourself:** Share the map (as an image or a text description of the steps) with an AI tool and ask it to identify steps where internal team perspective and user perspective commonly diverge in this type of experience. Use the output as a prompt list for your post-session validation — not as validation itself.
