# Using AI for Design Work
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 147 (AI as Execution Partner) | **Note:** Read before any method that has an "After you've run this yourself:" AI path. This piece is the foundational layer those paths build on.

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to use AI tools to accelerate design work — research synthesis, mapping, structuring, writing — without losing the judgment that makes the work useful.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of the last time you used AI to help with something and the output looked finished and confident — and then, when you tried to use it, something was off. Not obviously wrong; just not quite right for the specific situation. That gap between "looks right" and "is right for this" is the judgment gap this piece is about.

**Trigger:** You're about to use an AI tool for design-related work — synthesis, mapping, structuring, content writing — and want to know where AI genuinely accelerates the work versus where it produces a confident-looking result that misrepresents what you actually know.

**Why this works:** AI is a production-acceleration tool, not a judgment tool. It generates plausible-sounding structure from patterns in its training. Judgment evaluates whether that structure is correct for a specific situation — this organization, this user, this context, this constraint. These are different cognitive operations, and only one of them currently runs inside an AI model. The mistake is not using AI; it's letting AI do the judgment step because the output looks like it already has.

**Method:**
1. **For any design task, separate the production step from the judgment step.** Production is creating structure, drafting text, generating options, organizing raw material. Judgment is evaluating whether the structure is correct, deciding which option fits the constraint, verifying the draft reflects reality — not just pattern.
2. **Use AI for production steps.** "Generate a journey map skeleton for a user trying to [task] in [context]." "Give me 8 possible navigation groupings for these content types." "Rewrite this error message in plain language." These are production tasks — AI generates the structure or draft, which you then evaluate.
3. **Do the judgment steps yourself.** Is this journey map skeleton scoped to the right persona? Does this navigation grouping match what users actually call these things? Is this rewrite accurate or just plainer-sounding? These evaluation questions require knowledge of the specific situation that AI doesn't have — and you do.
4. **Watch for the red flags that AI has done the judgment step for you:**
   - The output is confident-sounding but you can't verify any specific claim against actual evidence.
   - The output contains specific details — user goals, pain points, navigation labels — that you didn't provide. (AI filled in gaps with plausible-seeming defaults that may not apply to your context.)
   - The output is polished enough to share without revision. (Polished AI output signals that something has been finished — but finished isn't the same as correct for your situation.)

**Artifact:** A clear boundary for one current design task: what you'll use AI to produce, and what judgment decision you'll make yourself after reviewing the output.

**Watchout:** The highest-risk AI output is the one that looks finished. A rough sketch signals uncertainty — you know it needs work. A polished AI-generated artifact signals confidence — and that confidence belongs to the artifact's form, not to the judgment behind its content. The more polished the output, the more important it is to do the judgment check.

**Try This:** Take one design task you're currently working on — a journey map, a navigation structure, a piece of interface copy, a research synthesis. Write the task as two parts: (1) what you'll ask AI to produce, and (2) what you'll verify or decide yourself after reviewing the output. Now run it and see whether the verification finds anything the production step got wrong.

**Proof:** If the AI output contains at least one specific claim that you can't verify against what you actually know about your situation — a user goal you didn't provide, a step in the journey you didn't know was part of the process, a grouping that sounds right but doesn't match how users describe their tasks — the production/judgment separation is doing its job. If you find no such claims, either the AI was given very specific context (good) or the output is so general it isn't useful yet (also useful to know).

**Take this further:** In the next week, run AI assistance on one design task with the production/judgment split explicitly written before you start. After reviewing the AI output, write one sentence: what did the AI fill in that you had to correct? That correction is what the judgment step is for.

**After you've run this yourself:** Review your last three "After you've run this yourself:" AI paths in any method you've completed. For each one, ask: did I do the judgment step, or did I accept the AI output as complete? Write one sentence about where the production/judgment line should have been clearer.
