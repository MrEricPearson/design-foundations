# Lightweight Research Mechanics — Arc Overview and Approach Selection
**Tier:** Arc Header | **Arc:** Lightweight Research Mechanics | **Prereq:** 115 (Listening), 131 (Question Bias), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude) | **Note:** Read this before choosing an approach piece.

**Arc Goal:** After completing the relevant approach piece in this arc, you will be able to gather real information about what users actually experience — using the approach that matches your access and the type of question you need to answer.

**Arc Trigger:** You need to understand something about real user experience, and all you currently have is what your team assumes.

---

## Choose Your Approach

The three research approaches differ in required access, question type they can answer, and what kind of data they produce. Choose based on your access first, then on whether your question is behavioral or attitudinal.

---

### Approach A — Conversational Interview → **202a**

**Use when:** You can get 20-30 minutes with someone who goes through the experience you're studying. The access is direct and synchronous.

**What you bring:** One open question that starts with the experience, not with your hypothesis. Time to listen.

**What you produce:** Direct quotes and specific descriptions of experience — what participants actually said, kept separate from your interpretation.

**The key judgment:** Whether your questions make it possible for the participant to contradict your assumptions. Open questions produce surprises; leading ones produce confirmation of what you already believe.

**Best for:** Attitudinal data (what people think, expect, believe) and self-report behavior (what people say they do). Pairs well with observational research when you want both.

→ **Read 202a (Conversational Interview)**

---

### Approach B — Remote / Async Research → **202b**

**Use when:** Participants aren't available synchronously — different time zones, heavy calendars, or a situation where written reflection produces better answers than real-time conversation.

**What you bring:** A set of carefully sequenced questions designed to be self-contained. No follow-up is possible in async; the question sequence has to do the work that probing does in a conversation.

**What you produce:** Written responses that you can analyze without real-time access to the participant.

**The key judgment:** Whether the questions are specific enough to produce useful answers without clarification. Ambiguous async questions produce unhelpful answers that can't be followed up.

**Best for:** Attitudinal data and reflective self-report. Less reliable for behavioral accuracy — people reflect on what they did, rather than describing it in context.

→ **Read 202b (Remote / Async Research)**

---

### Approach C — Observational Research → **202c**

**Use when:** You need behavioral data — what people actually do rather than what they say they do. You have access to the context where the behavior happens: a session, a workspace, a task being performed.

**What you bring:** Permission to observe and a commitment to writing what you see, not what you interpret.

**What you produce:** Behavior notes: specific descriptions of what happened, separate from any interpretation about why.

**The key judgment:** The difference between a behavior note and an interpretation note. "She clicked the back button twice without reading the confirmation message" is a behavior. "She seemed confused" is an interpretation. Only behavior notes survive synthesis.

**Best for:** Behavioral data. Catches what people don't think to mention in interviews because they've normalized it.

→ **Read 202c (Observational Research)**

---

## When You Can't Reach Participants

**If you have no access to actual participants:** Don't attempt a primary research method without participants. Instead:

- **Support tickets and help desk logs** — what participants have already told someone when they were frustrated enough to report it. Incomplete but directional.
- **Analytics and usage data** — behavioral data without the context. Shows what happened, not why.
- **Secondary research** — published research on similar user populations, similar problems, or comparable products.

Label any findings from these sources explicitly: "derived from support logs," not "research finding." The labeling is not pedantry — it's what prevents secondhand evidence from being treated as direct evidence in subsequent decisions.

**This is not a degraded version of primary research.** It is a different input type with a different confidence level. Run it, label it accurately, and plan for primary research when access becomes available.
