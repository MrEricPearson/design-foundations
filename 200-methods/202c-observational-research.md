# 202c — Observational Research
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Lightweight Research Mechanics (see 202-research-arc.md) | **Prereqs:** 115 (Listening), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude), 156 (Name What People Do, Not What They Think) | **Note:** Use when you need behavioral data. Pairs with 214 (Affinity Mapping).

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to capture what users actually do — in the context where they do it — rather than what they say they do.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of a process you thought you understood until you watched someone go through it. The gap between the verbal description and the observed behavior is what this method exists to capture. People normalize their workarounds; they don't report them in interviews because they've stopped noticing them.

**Trigger:** You need behavioral data — what people do, in context — rather than attitudinal data about what people think, prefer, or say they do. You have access to a session, a workspace, or a task being performed in its natural context.

**Why this works:** People can't accurately self-report behaviors they've automated. The workaround they've been doing for six months doesn't show up in an interview because it's invisible to them — they stopped noticing it. Observation catches what self-report misses: the actual sequence of steps, the moments of hesitation, the places where the environment compensates for what the product doesn't do.

**Method:**
1. **Define what behavior you're there to observe.** Before arriving (or before the session starts), write the specific task or process you're watching. Not "how do they use the product" — that's too broad. "How do they complete [specific task] when [specific context applies]."
2. **Write what you see, not what you infer.** Your notes should describe actions, not states. "She opened a second browser tab and copied the confirmation number into a text document before clicking submit" is a behavior. "She seemed uncertain about the system" is an interpretation. The behavior note is the evidence; the interpretation is the claim derived from it. Keep them separate.
3. **Note pauses, workarounds, and self-corrections.** These are the most behaviorally informative moments. A pause before clicking something is a moment of uncertainty. A workaround is a sign that the product doesn't do what the user needs. A self-correction is a sign that feedback from the product is absent or delayed.
4. **Don't intervene.** If you're observing someone use a product, don't explain or assist. What you'd explain or assist with is precisely what the observation was designed to surface. Write it down instead.

**Artifact:** A set of behavior notes — specific, observable descriptions of what happened during the observed session, kept separate from any interpretation.

**Watchout:** Observation doesn't produce why. It produces what. If you need to understand the reasoning behind a behavior, a follow-up conversation after the observation session is the right method — not interpretation of the behavior itself during the session.

**When You Can't Run the Full Version**

**If you can observe but not observe in context (the actual work environment):** Observe a simulated version of the task instead — the participant performs the task in a controlled setting rather than their natural environment. You lose environmental context (the tools, interruptions, and resources that affect real behavior) but gain access to the task sequence. Label the findings: "observed in simulated context."

**What you still get:** Behavioral data. The task sequence, the pauses, the workarounds, and the moments of hesitation are all visible even in a simulated context.

**What you give up:** Environmental factors that may be causing or masking the behavior. A workaround that exists because of an environmental constraint won't appear if that constraint isn't present in the simulated context.

**Don't do this:** Don't substitute a retrospective interview about past behavior for observational data when behavioral accuracy is the research need. Retrospective accounts of behavior are self-report data, not observational data — no matter how recent the behavior was.

**Try This:** Find one task a user performs that you think you understand. Watch one person do it. Write only what you observe — actions and sequences, no interpretations. Count how many steps you didn't know were part of the process.

**Proof:** If the behavior notes contain steps, pauses, or workarounds you didn't know existed before the observation, the method worked. If the notes only describe what you expected to see, either the task is genuinely well-understood or your note-taking slipped from behavior description into expectation confirmation. Check: can every note be verified against a recording of the session? If not, an interpretation crept in.

**Take this further:** In the next week, find one behavior note from the observation and ask: why might someone do this? Write three possible explanations. Then plan one follow-up question to find out which explanation is closest to correct. Write one sentence about what question the observation generated that you didn't think to ask before watching.

**After you've run this yourself:** Describe the behavior you observed (the specific sequence, the workarounds) to an AI tool and ask it to generate hypotheses for why. Treat the output as a hypothesis list for your follow-up conversation — not as conclusions.

**What Next:** If the observation generated multiple behavior notes to find patterns in, read 214 (Affinity Mapping). If you want to understand the reasoning behind what you observed, follow up with 202a (Conversational Interview). To connect behavioral findings to a persona, take them to 301 (From Vague Ask to Real Persona).
