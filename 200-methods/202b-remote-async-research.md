# 202b — Remote / Async Research
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Lightweight Research Mechanics (see 202-research-arc.md) | **Prereqs:** 115 (Listening), 131 (Question Bias), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude) | **Note:** Use when synchronous access isn't available. Pairs with 214 (Affinity Mapping) for synthesis.

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to gather useful research input from participants who aren't available for a live conversation — without the ambiguity that makes most async questions produce unhelpful answers.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of a question you've sent someone that came back with a general, polished answer when you wanted something specific. The specificity problem in async research is usually a question design problem — the question didn't give the participant enough structure to produce the answer you needed.

**Trigger:** You need to understand something about real user experience, but participants aren't available synchronously — different time zones, high-demand calendars, or a situation where reflection produces better input than real-time conversation.

**Why this works:** Async research shifts the probing responsibility from the live conversation to the question sequence itself. In a conversation, you can follow unexpected threads and ask follow-ups. In async, the question sequence has to do that work in advance. Well-designed async questions front-load the specificity that live probing usually provides.

**Method:**
1. **Define the one thing you most need to know.** Write it as "Assuming: ___." This is your anchor. Every question in the sequence serves this one research need — not five parallel needs.
2. **Design a three-question sequence.** Start with a concrete, recent example: "Think about the last time you [did X]. Describe what happened — specifically what you did and what you were trying to accomplish." The first question establishes the specific instance. Don't ask about general behavior; ask about the last actual time.
3. **Second question: probe the specific account.** "You mentioned [something from question 1]. Walk me through that part in more detail." This question doesn't exist yet — you fill it in after reading the first response. If you're sending all questions at once (before you see responses), anticipate the part of the account most relevant to your research need and probe there.
4. **Third question: the assumption check.** "What would have made [the part they described in question 1] easier or clearer?" This is the question that tests your assumption without stating it. If your assumption is wrong, this question surfaces that.
5. **Wait for responses before interpreting.** Don't draw conclusions until you have full responses. Partial reads of async responses tend to pattern-match to existing assumptions.

**Artifact:** Written responses from one or more participants — specific accounts of their experience, held separately from your interpretations of what those accounts mean.

**Watchout:** Ambiguous async questions produce unusable responses that can't be followed up. The test: if the question can be answered with "it depends" or a one-sentence general statement, it's too open. Add "the last time specifically" or "describe what happened" to anchor it to a concrete instance.

**When You Can't Run the Full Version**

**If you can only get one question answered:** Ask the first question from the three-question sequence only. "Think about the last time you [did X]. Describe what happened — specifically what you did and what you were trying to accomplish." One specific account is more useful than no account.

**What you still get:** One anchored account of real experience. This is qualitatively different from assumption, even without the follow-up depth.

**What you give up:** The ability to probe. You'll have the story but not the ability to ask about the part of it that surprised you.

**Don't do this:** Don't send a survey with 10 questions when you need depth from 1 question. Survey tools produce survey behavior — participants answer quickly and generally. Async questions sent as messages or email produce more reflective responses.

**Try This:** Write your assumption as "Assuming: ___." Write the first question of the three-question sequence using a real scenario from your current work. Send it to one person. Read their response before writing the second question — don't pre-write the full sequence if you can avoid it.

**Proof:** If the first response contains something specific you didn't anticipate — a detail, a sequence, a choice — the question structure worked. If the response is general ("I usually just..."), the question didn't anchor to a concrete instance. Rewrite the question with "the last time specifically."

**Take this further:** In the next week, run the full three-question sequence with one additional participant. Compare the accounts. Write one sentence about what differed between participants that you hadn't expected — that difference is often where the real research value is.

**After you've run this yourself:** Paste your three-question sequence into an AI tool and ask it to check each question for whether it could be answered with a general statement. Have it suggest a more specific anchor for any question that fails the check. Review its suggestions against your actual research need.

**What Next:** If you have multiple async responses to find patterns in, read 214 (Affinity Mapping). To connect findings to a persona, take them to 301 (From Vague Ask to Real Persona). For behavioral data — what people actually do rather than describe — read 202c (Observational Research).
