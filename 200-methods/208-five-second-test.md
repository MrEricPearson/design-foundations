# Five-Second Test / First-Click Test
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Standalone

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to find out what your design communicates first — in five seconds, with one person, right now.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of something you showed someone and they said they liked it — but later couldn't remember what it was for. Or the reverse: you showed something rough and they instantly understood the purpose. That moment of instant comprehension (or its absence) is what a five-second test measures.

**Trigger:** you want a fast read on whether something communicates clearly, without a full research study.

**Why this works:** what someone retains in the first five seconds is a direct readout of what the design communicates most loudly — not what it says most carefully. Hierarchy problems, unclear labeling, and competing visual weight all show up in what gets remembered and what doesn't.

**Method:** show someone the design for five seconds (or ask where they'd click first), then ask what they remember or where they went. Compare that to what you intended.

**Artifact:** a quick gap (or match) between what you intended and what someone actually understood.

**Watchout:** this tells you about first impressions, not deep usability — don't treat a good five-second result as full validation.

**Try This:** show something you're working on to one person for five seconds, then cover it and ask: "What did you see first, and what do you think this is for?" Compare their answer to your intent.

**Proof:** What someone remembers after five seconds is a direct measure of what your design communicated first. If it matches your intent, you have confirmation. If it doesn't, you have a specific gap — not a vague feeling that something might be off.

**Take this further:** in the next few days, show the same design to two more people for five seconds each. Write one sentence: was the gap consistent across all three, or did it vary? Consistency means a structural problem; variation means something else is at play.

**What Next:** if the five-second result reveals a hierarchy problem (they saw the wrong thing first), read 133 (Visual Hierarchy). If you want to test whether users can actually complete a task — not just what they notice — run 215a (Moderated Usability Session) for a live session, or 215b (Unmoderated Usability Testing) if participants work independently.
