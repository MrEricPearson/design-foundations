# Affinity Mapping
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 155 (Synthesis vs. Analysis), 156 (Name What People Do, Not What They Think), any data-gathering piece (202a/202b/202c, 208, 211) or workshop (304) | **Note:** Pairs with 202 research arc, 301 persona arc

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to find behavioral patterns in raw data without losing the individual observations that produced them.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of the last time you debriefed after research or a workshop — and the conversation went in circles because different people remembered different things, or kept arguing about which interpretation was right before anyone agreed on what the data actually said.

**Trigger:** you have raw notes from interviews, a workshop, or a review session and need to find patterns — without losing the individual data points in a summary.

**Why this works:** affinity mapping separates observation from grouping and grouping from naming — three steps that happen simultaneously in most debriefs, which is why debriefs produce arguments instead of findings. Doing them in sequence makes interpretation visible and arguable rather than baked into someone's summary.

**Method:**
1. Write each observation, quote, or finding on its own note — one idea per note, verbatim where possible. Don't interpret yet.
2. Distribute all notes in view where you and anyone else involved can see them simultaneously. Digital or physical — either works.
3. Group silently. No discussion until grouping is complete. Move notes that feel related without explaining why. The silence prevents premature framing — any early label becomes a filter on everything that follows.
4. Once grouping stops changing, name each cluster. Label with behavior, not category: "users restart rather than fix" rather than "errors." The behavior label describes what you found; the category label only describes a topic.
5. Review: are there notes that don't fit anywhere? These are often the most important findings — the things you didn't predict.

**Artifact:** a labeled cluster map where every raw observation is still visible, organized by behavioral theme, with outliers explicitly preserved.

**Watchout:** affinity mapping produces one interpretation of your data — whoever grouped it shaped it. With a single researcher, it reflects one person's pattern recognition. The method's strength is making that interpretation visible and arguable, not making it objective. Treat the map as a hypothesis to discuss, not a finding to report.

**Try This:** take the last set of notes you gathered from any source — a meeting, an interview, a user session. Write each point on a separate sticky or doc line. Spend 10 minutes grouping them silently. Then name each cluster with a behavior statement. Do the cluster names describe anything you didn't already know?

**Proof:** If you end up with cluster names you didn't expect — behavior patterns you weren't looking for when you gathered the data — the method worked. If every cluster name was predictable from what you knew before the session, either the data confirmed your hypotheses strongly or you shaped the groupings to fit what you expected. The silent grouping step is what prevents the latter.

**Take this further:** in the next week, share the cluster map with someone who wasn't in the grouping session. Write one sentence: what did they read differently — and did their reading change any of the cluster names?

**After you've run this yourself:** paste your raw notes into an AI tool and ask for an initial grouping by behavioral theme — then reorganize it yourself, treating the output as a first draft to argue with. The AI gives you a starting structure; your pattern recognition over the actual data is the method.

**At Enterprise Scale**

When research rounds generate 400-600 raw observations — a common volume for enterprise research programs with multiple stakeholder groups — the standard affinity mapping method breaks down mechanically. Physical sticky notes aren't feasible at that volume, and even digital tools become unwieldy when you're trying to view hundreds of notes simultaneously.

The judgment stays the same: name behavioral patterns from the evidence, not from the hypothesis. What changes is the mechanics:

**Step 1 — Stratified sampling.** Before clustering, sort your observations by source (each participant, each session, each team interviewed). Select a representative set from each source — 8-12 observations that capture the range of what that source produced. A 600-observation dataset becomes 80-120 representative observations. This is not cherry-picking: you're reducing volume while preserving source diversity.

**Step 2 — AI-assisted first-pass clustering.** Paste the representative observations into an AI tool and ask for an initial grouping by behavioral theme. Use the output as a starting structure to reorganize, not as the result. Treat every AI-generated cluster name as a hypothesis — check it against the observations you have, rename what doesn't fit, and split clusters that combine distinct behaviors.

**Step 3 — Human validation of cluster names.** For each cluster, go back to the full observation set and ask: does any observation from outside my sample belong here? Any cluster name that breaks when you check it against the full set needs revision.

The enterprise version produces the same artifact — a labeled cluster map with preserved observations — but the path to it uses sampling and AI-assisted structuring as intermediate steps. The critical judgment remains: every cluster name describes behavior, not category.
