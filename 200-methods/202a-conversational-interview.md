# 202a — Conversational Interview
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Lightweight Research Mechanics (see 202-research-arc.md) | **Prereqs:** 115 (Listening), 131 (Question Bias), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude) | **Note:** Use when you have 20-30 min synchronous access. Prereq for 301 (Persona arc); pairs with 214 (Affinity Mapping).

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to conduct a short research conversation that gives you real quotes — not your own assumptions restated.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of a decision you made recently that turned out to be wrong about what users needed or wanted. That gap between what you assumed and what was true is what this method exists to close — before you commit, not after.

**Trigger:** You need to understand someone's real experience and can get 20-30 minutes with them. You have a specific question about what they actually do or think — not what you hope they do.

**Why this works:** The difference between a leading question and an open one isn't tone — it's whether the question makes it possible for the participant to contradict you. Open questions produce surprises; leading ones produce confirmation of what you already believe. A conversation that confirms every assumption you brought into it is not research — it's social validation.

**Method:**
1. **Write your assumption first.** Before the conversation, write: "I'm currently assuming ___." This is the thing you most want to find out if you're wrong about. The conversation is designed to check it.
2. **Open with their experience, not your hypothesis.** Start with a question about the last time they did the relevant thing: "Walk me through the last time you had to [do the task]." This anchors the conversation in something real rather than something imagined.
3. **Follow their thread.** When they mention something unexpected, ask them to tell you more about that specifically — before returning to your planned questions. The unexpected thing is often the most useful thing.
4. **Write what they said, not what you concluded.** Keep two separate spaces: direct quotes and paraphrase on one side, your interpretations on the other. The separation matters — it's what lets you tell the difference between what you heard and what you inferred.

**Artifact:** Notes from the conversation: direct quotes and specific descriptions of experience, kept separate from your own conclusions.

**Watchout:** One conversation is not a pattern — it's a data point. When findings will drive a major product direction, multiple rounds are needed, or the stakes are high, flag it to a Researcher: they add structural rigor (research plan, screened participants, auditable synthesis) that this method can't provide.

**When You Can't Run the Full Version**

**If you can only get 10 minutes:** Reduce to one question. "Walk me through the last time you [did X]." Write what they say verbatim. One specific story from 10 minutes is more useful than a general impression from 30.

**What you still get:** One direct account of real experience — which is qualitatively different from assumption.

**What you give up:** The ability to follow unexpected threads or check a second assumption. The depth that comes from a participant who's warmed up mid-conversation.

**Don't do this:** Don't replace the conversation with a survey when your question requires understanding context, sequence, or why. Surveys answer "how often" and "how many." They don't produce the specific accounts that change how you understand a problem.

**Try This:** Find one belief you currently hold about your users and write it as "Assuming: ___." Write the open question you'd ask to find out if you're right. Schedule 20 minutes with one person and ask it. Start with their last time doing the thing.

**Proof:** If the notes contain things you didn't expect to hear — accounts that contradict or complicate your assumption — the method worked. If everything in the notes confirms what you already believed, either you found strong validation or you asked leading questions. The separation between quotes and conclusions is what lets you tell the difference afterward.

**Take this further:** In the next few days, go back to one note from the conversation. Ask: did anything I heard change a decision I was already making? Write one sentence about what shifted, or what held. That reflection is the research earning its keep.

**After you've run this yourself:** Use an AI tool to review your next set of interview questions for bias — paste the questions and ask whether any assume the answer before the participant confirms it. Treat the output as a checklist to review, not a replacement for your own judgment about what questions serve your research goal.

**What Next:** If the conversation gave you multiple observations to find patterns in, read 214 (Affinity Mapping). If you want to connect the findings to a persona, take them to 301 (From Vague Ask to Real Persona). If you need behavioral data — what people actually do rather than say they do — read 202c (Observational Research).
