# Structured Ideation / Crazy 8s
**Tier:** 200 — Practice | **Arc:** Standalone | **Note:** Candidate technique inside 304 (Workshop arc); pairs with 107 (Framing), 128 (HMW)

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to generate eight rough ideas in eight minutes — and stop defaulting to the first thing that comes to mind.

**Prior knowledge hook:** Think of the last time you or a team proposed "the solution" and then spent the rest of the meeting defending it instead of exploring others. The first idea that survives a room isn't necessarily the best one — it's often just the most articulate one.

**Trigger:** you (or a group) are stuck on the first idea and haven't generated real alternatives.

**Why this works:** the time limit forces abandonment of evaluation — you can't generate eight ideas in eight minutes and simultaneously judge which is best. The quantity is the mechanism: the seventh and eighth ideas are almost always different from the first one, and different is where the value is.

**Method:** set a short timer. Sketch as many different rough ideas as you can in that window — quantity over quality. Don't evaluate yet, just generate.

**Artifact:** several distinct rough options instead of one polished one.

**Watchout:** this only works if evaluation is genuinely deferred — judging ideas as they're generated kills the volume this method depends on.

**When You Can't Run the Full Version**

**If the room is already anchored on a solution:** Crazy 8s run in a room that's already converged on a direction produces performative divergence — participants sketch variations on the predetermined idea, and the room returns to where it started. The method fails not because it was executed incorrectly but because the conditions for it to work weren't established.

Detect pre-convergence before attempting the method: ask the room "if we had to pick a direction right now, what would it be?" If most people name the same direction, the room is already anchored. Running Crazy 8s on an anchored room without first creating space for genuine alternatives wastes the session.

**What to do instead:** Before Crazy 8s, run a brief de-anchoring step. Name the direction explicitly ("it seems we're assuming X"). Assign someone to generate ideas that contradict that direction specifically ("sketch an approach where X is NOT the solution"). Only then run Crazy 8s — with the anti-X ideas already on the table as permission to diverge.

**What you still get:** The quantity and time-pressure mechanisms of the method, applied to a room that's been given explicit permission to generate real alternatives.

**What you give up:** The natural divergence that emerges when the room hasn't pre-converged. The de-anchoring adds a step that shouldn't be necessary in an open generative session.

**Don't do this:** Don't run Crazy 8s in a room where the decision has already been made and the goal is documentation, not exploration. When the purpose of the session is to generate buy-in for a predetermined direction, Crazy 8s introduces divergence that will only be dismissed, damaging trust in the method for future use.

**At Enterprise Scale**

With groups larger than 8-10, all participants sketching simultaneously and then sharing produces a debrief that's too long for the time budget and loses energy. Structure large-group Crazy 8s in parallel sub-groups of 4-5, each running the 8-minute timer simultaneously, then sharing within the sub-group and selecting 2 most different ideas to bring to the full group. The full group sees 2 ideas per sub-group — a manageable set — and converges from there.

**Try This:** set a timer for 8 minutes. Sketch one idea per minute for your current problem — eight ideas, eight minutes. When the timer ends, lay them out and identify the two most different from each other. Those two are where the real options begin.

**Proof:** If the output looks like one idea with small variations, evaluation crept back in. The two most different ideas in your set are the signal that deferred judgment worked — they couldn't both come from the same creative starting point.

**Take this further:** in the next few days, take the two most different ideas you generated and sketch one screen each. Which one surfaced a tradeoff you hadn't named before? Write one sentence about it.

**After you've run this yourself:** ask an AI tool for eight brief descriptions of different approaches to your problem — one sentence each, no evaluation — then sketch each one yourself. AI gives you the divergence; your sketches make them real.

**What Next:** once you have several options, you need to pick one direction — read 109 (Weighing Trade-offs) to evaluate them. If you want to develop one direction further before committing, read 127 (Storyboarding).
