# Selection Bias
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 100 (Assumption vs. Fact), 261 (Correlation vs. Causation) | **Cluster:** K — Data Literacy

**Goal:** Recognize when the users, sessions, or data points in your analysis are a systematically non-representative sample — and identify who or what is missing.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that if you have enough data about users who did something, you can draw conclusions about users in general. The correction: the people in your data are the people who ended up in your data — which is almost always a subset of all the people you care about, and the selection process that produced that subset is rarely random. Data about users who completed the onboarding flow is data about users who completed the onboarding flow, not about users who started it. Data from users who opted into a beta is data about willing early adopters, not about the eventual full user population.

The mechanism: any time someone or something decides who appears in your dataset, that decision produces a selected sample rather than a random one. Selection can happen because of who chooses to participate (users who opt in to a study), who is retained in the system (users who don't churn), who completes a flow (users who didn't drop off), or who responds to a survey (users who care enough to respond, which correlates with opinions stronger than average). In each case, the population in the data is systematically different from the population you want to know about. Findings about the selected sample may be accurate; conclusions applied to the broader population are extrapolating beyond the data's actual scope.

**You'll see it when:** Survey results from engaged users are used to infer what "users want." Usability test findings from recruited volunteers are applied to first-time users who never signed up for testing. Retention analysis based on users still active at month 6 describes those users, not the original cohort including everyone who left before month 6.

**The signal:** The claim being made is about a population broader than the data's actual scope. The collection method for the data gave some people a higher chance of appearing in it than others — and the people with higher chances are systematically different on the dimension being studied. The question "who is missing from this data?" produces a meaningful answer.

**Don't confuse this with:** Small sample size, which is about uncertainty from quantity. Selection bias is about systematic distortion from collection process, not uncertainty from quantity. A large biased sample can produce confident wrong conclusions; a small unbiased sample can produce uncertain but correctly-aimed ones. The false positive: if the selected group happens to match the broader population on the dimension being studied, selection bias exists in the method but doesn't affect the conclusion. The concern is when who got selected is correlated with what you're trying to measure.

**Try Noticing:** Think about the last research or analytics finding you encountered. Who is in the data? How did they get there? Write down who would not appear in this data — users who never engaged, users who tried and quit, users who were never recruited, users in populations not captured by the collection method. If the people missing from the data are different on the dimension being studied, the finding applies to the included group but may not generalize.
