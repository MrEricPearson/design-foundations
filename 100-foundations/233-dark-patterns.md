# Dark Patterns
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 229, 230, 231 | **Episode:** 4

**Goal:** Recognize design patterns that use cognitive bias against users' own goals, so you can identify them in work you inherit and avoid introducing them in work you produce.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that dark patterns are dramatic, obvious manipulations — spam subscriptions buried in checkout flows, hidden unsubscribe buttons. The correction: most dark patterns are subtle enough that practitioners introduce them without awareness, because the same design moves that make a legitimate experience cleaner can also make a manipulative one harder to resist.

The mechanism: dark patterns are design decisions that work against the user's stated goals by exploiting the cognitive biases they cannot consciously override. They exploit loss aversion by creating artificial urgency ("only 2 left") that makes inaction feel like a loss. They exploit status quo bias by setting defaults that serve the product's interests over the user's. They exploit anchoring by placing high-priced options first to make subsequent options feel cheap. They exploit social proof by displaying inflated or fabricated usage counts that manufacture apparent consensus.

The critical distinction: dark patterns work by harnessing a real cognitive mechanism against the user's own interests. Legitimate persuasion also uses cognitive mechanisms — but in service of helping users do something they actually want to do. The mechanism is not what makes something a dark pattern. Whether it works against the user's goals is what makes something a dark pattern.

Named types worth recognizing: confirm-shaming (making the decline option feel like a character judgment); roach motel (easy to enter, hard to exit); hidden costs (prices revealed only at checkout); disguised ads (ads presented as organic results or recommendations); misdirection (drawing attention away from the action the user would choose if paying attention).

**You'll see it when:** A design makes the path the product wants users to take significantly easier than the path users would choose if fully informed and unhurried. Or when the decline or cancel option is visually minimized, requires additional steps, or uses language designed to create guilt.

**The signal:** A user would choose differently if they had more time, more information, or the layout were neutral. The design is exploiting the gap between what a hurried user does and what an informed user would choose.

**Don't confuse this with:** Legitimate choice architecture. Default settings, simplified flows, and recommended options are not inherently dark patterns — these can genuinely help users do what they want more easily. The test: does this design help the user accomplish their own goal, or does it redirect them toward a goal that serves the product at the user's expense? Read 234 (Persuasion vs. Manipulation) for this distinction in detail.

**Try Noticing:** Look at a checkout, subscription, or cancellation flow in a product you use regularly. Count how many steps the desired action (purchase/subscribe) requires versus the avoided action (cancel/decline). Is the language on the decline option neutral or charged? Would a user who was paying full attention choose differently than a user moving quickly?
