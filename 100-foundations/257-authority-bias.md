# Authority Bias
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 247, 149 | **Cluster:** J — Cognitive Science

**Goal:** Recognize when source credibility — rather than argument quality — is determining whether a finding or recommendation is accepted or dismissed.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that the quality of an argument determines whether it lands — that a well-reasoned recommendation with solid evidence will be accepted on its merits. The correction: System 1 uses source credibility as a heuristic for argument quality. The same claim from a named expert, a senior title, or an external authority is processed differently than the same claim from an unknown or junior source. S1 makes this evaluation before S2 engages with the argument content — credibility signals shape the evaluation, not just the reception.

The mechanism: in contexts of uncertainty (which includes most design decisions), S1 defaults to source as a proxy for reliability. This is rational collective intelligence — calibrating trust based on track record and expertise is often correct. But it means that research findings presented without credibility signals (methodology, participant count, who conducted it, what the finding is specifically) get evaluated by S1 as "someone's opinion," and that "someone's opinion" from a senior stakeholder outweighs documented evidence from an unknown junior researcher. The argument's logical structure is almost irrelevant until source credibility is established.

**You'll see it when:** A well-documented finding from a junior team member is overridden by an undocumented assertion from a senior stakeholder. The same recommendation that failed to move a decision gets traction after being attributed to an external expert or consultant. Research findings presented as "we found" produce less buy-in than the same findings presented with methodology, sample size, and specific quote.

**The signal:** The decision changed — or didn't change — based on who said it, not what was said. Questions like "who did this research?" or "has anyone senior reviewed this?" appearing before questions about the methodology or findings are authority bias signals.

**Don't confuse this with:** Appropriate deference to expertise. Authority Bias names the mechanism; whether it's operating appropriately in a specific context is a separate judgment. A surgeon's recommendation during surgery is appropriate authority deference; that same surgeon's recommendation about an interface design problem is authority bias at work. The false positive: a finding that gained traction after being endorsed by a senior leader appears to have been accepted on merit — the endorsement may have been the actual cause, not the argument.

**Try Noticing:** In the next meeting where a research finding or recommendation is presented, notice whether the first questions are about the source ("who did this?", "what's their background?") or the content ("what was the methodology?", "how many participants?"). The order of those questions reveals which axis S1 is evaluating on first. Also notice when a decision shifts after a title or seniority is established — not after new information is added.
