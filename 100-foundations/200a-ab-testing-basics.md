# A/B Testing Basics
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 103, 113 | **Note:** Bridges prototyping to live validation; prereq for testing/validation arc when developed; file numbered 200a to avoid conflict with 200-series methods

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to recognize when A/B testing is the right method for a question — and when it's being used to defer a decision that should have been made before anything was built.

**Concept:** A/B testing runs two or more versions of the same thing simultaneously with real users under real conditions, measuring which version produces the desired outcome. It is the only method that measures actual behavior at scale with statistical confidence — not what users say they prefer, not what looks better to the team, but what users do when they don't know they're being tested.

This precision comes with a specific limitation: A/B testing tells you which version won, not why. The winning version may have won for a reason you didn't intend. A blue button may outperform a green button because of contrast ratio, because blue signals trustworthiness, or because the winning cohort happened to have more mobile users who tap faster. The test tells you the outcome; the interpretation of the outcome is your work.

The mechanism that makes A/B testing valuable is also what makes it easy to misuse: it produces numbers, and numbers feel objective. A statistically significant result at 95% confidence feels like certainty. It isn't — it is the result that probability-of-chance is below 5%, which still means 1 in 20 significant results is false. More practically: a test that was designed to measure the wrong thing will produce statistically significant results about the wrong thing, and those results will be used to make decisions with misplaced confidence.

**You'll see it when:** A design decision is being tested in production with real traffic, measuring a real behavioral outcome (click rate, conversion, task completion) across two or more versions. This is appropriate when: sufficient traffic exists to reach statistical significance in a reasonable timeframe, the question is behavioral (not conceptual), and the versions being tested represent a real decision rather than a superficial one. A test between two button labels on a low-traffic page may take six months to reach significance — during which both versions perform at unknown quality.

**The signal:** A number is being treated as the final answer to a design question, without asking what the number measured and whether that measurement is a proxy for what actually matters. "Version B had 12% higher click rate" is a measurement. "Version B is the better design" is an inference that requires knowing whether click rate is what matters, whether both versions had the same downstream conversion, and whether the increase is meaningful at the scale of the business.

**Don't confuse A/B testing with user testing** — user testing reveals why users behave a certain way; A/B testing measures whether they do. Both are measurement, but at different fidelity on different questions. A/B testing cannot tell you why the losing version lost. A participant in a usability session can. When you need to understand the mechanism, user testing is the method. When you've understood the mechanism and need to measure outcomes at scale, A/B testing is the method.

**Try Noticing:** Think about a decision your team has made based on A/B test results. What was measured (the metric)? What was being decided (the real question)? Are those the same thing? If the metric is a proxy for the real question — clicks as a proxy for satisfaction, page views as a proxy for value — ask whether the proxy is reliable. A proxy can be optimized independently of the real outcome.

**What Next:** A/B testing lives at the end of the validation sequence: after the concept is sound (prototyping), after the experience is tested with real users (usability testing), and after the design is implemented in a form that real users can interact with. Testing anything before those prior questions are answered produces statistically rigorous results about the wrong thing.
