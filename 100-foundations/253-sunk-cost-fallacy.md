# Sunk Cost Fallacy
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 103, 247 | **Cluster:** J — Cognitive Science

**Goal:** Recognize when sunk cost reasoning — not current value — is driving a decision to continue with a direction, so you can separate investment from merit in your own thinking and in team conversations.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that smart, self-aware people don't fall for the sunk cost fallacy — that only poor reasoners continue a bad investment because of prior cost. The correction: sunk cost reasoning is not irrational from System 1's perspective. Past investment signals that a path was chosen for reasons. Abandoning it requires declaring that those reasons were wrong — which is cognitively expensive, socially costly, and threatens consistency of identity. S1 produces "we've come this far" not as a logical error but as a protection mechanism.

The mechanism: this is distinct from 103 (Attachment), which describes the emotional identification with a direction you built or championed. Sunk Cost Fallacy names the *economic reasoning pattern* — "we've invested too much to stop now" — that operates even after attachment is acknowledged and even for things you didn't personally build. The economic frame ("we already spent X") functions as an argument, not just a feeling, which makes it more durable than emotional attachment and harder to surface through self-reflection alone. Sunk cost reasoning can coexist with explicit awareness of the fallacy: a team can agree "we shouldn't make decisions based on sunk costs" and then do exactly that, because the reasoning doesn't present itself as sunk cost reasoning — it presents itself as prudence.

**You'll see it when:** A direction is defended primarily in terms of what has already been invested: "we've built too much to change now," "we already committed to this roadmap," "we've spent months on this approach." The argument is about prior investment, not current value. Or: alternatives are dismissed not on their merits but on the switching cost — what would need to be rebuilt, relearned, or re-explained if the direction changed.

**The signal:** The primary argument for continuing is backward-looking: time, money, effort, or credibility already spent. Absent the prior investment, the same argument for continuing would not hold up. The test: if we were starting from scratch today with the same information, would we choose this direction? If yes, prior investment is irrelevant to the decision. If no, sunk cost reasoning may be the actual driver.

**Don't confuse this with:** Reasonable consideration of switching costs, which is forward-looking — the time and cost required to change direction is a real current cost that belongs in any decision. The distinction: switching cost is a future cost that factors into whether change is worth it; sunk cost is a past investment that doesn't factor into current value. The false positive: a team that continues in a direction because switching costs are genuinely prohibitive looks identical to a team continuing because of sunk cost reasoning; the test is whether the continuation decision would look the same if the switching costs were the only factor, without any reference to prior investment.

**Try Noticing:** In the next discussion about whether to continue, change, or stop something, notice which arguments are backward-looking (about what's already been spent) and which are forward-looking (about what's gained or lost from here). A meeting that spends most of its time on what's already been invested before asking whether the direction is still right is running on sunk cost logic.

**What Next:** 103 (Attachment) for the emotional complement — the identification with a direction that makes stopping feel like personal failure. 109 (Weighing Tradeoffs) for the forward-looking evaluation that sunk cost reasoning displaces. 315 (Psychology of Resistance) for the arc where sunk cost appears as a compound with Loss Aversion in organizational change contexts.
