# Reciprocity
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 247, 129 | **Cluster:** J — Cognitive Science

**Goal:** Recognize when reciprocity — the automatic social obligation created by receiving something of value — is available as a mechanism for building collaborative relationships, and when it's absent as an explanation for why relationships feel transactional.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that giving something to someone is transactional — it creates an obligation they can choose to honor or ignore based on their own calculation. The correction: receiving something of genuine value produces an automatic, uncomfortable sense of obligation to reciprocate — this is System 1, not deliberate social calculation. Reciprocity is a deeply embedded social norm that operates before deliberation. The discomfort of an unreciprocated debt is its own motivator, independent of whether the person decides they want to cooperate.

The mechanism: S1 tracks social exchange as a running balance. When the balance is in deficit — when someone gave something and received nothing back — S1 flags the imbalance as a relationship problem requiring resolution. This flagging is experienced as discomfort, as a sense of obligation, or as heightened motivation to help. Crucially, reciprocity operates on genuine giving. Manufactured or performative giving is recognized as such, and triggers Reactance rather than obligation. The gift must be real — something the receiver actually valued — for reciprocity to activate. What counts as "real" is determined by the receiver, not the giver.

In practitioner contexts, reciprocity shows up as: sharing relevant knowledge before asking for a stakeholder's time; providing a useful critique or perspective before requesting one; solving a small problem for someone before asking for a larger favor. These aren't transactional moves — they're the sequencing that changes the social context of the subsequent request.

**You'll see it when:** Stakeholders who received something useful from you before you needed anything from them are more responsive and more collaborative than stakeholders who only hear from you when you need something. Requests made in the context of a relationship where value has already been exchanged receive a different quality of engagement than cold requests. Relationships where one side only takes feel extractive to the other side — not as a reasoned judgment but as an automatic social response.

**The signal:** The relationship pattern between giving and receiving is imbalanced in a single direction: one person consistently asks for things without having previously provided something of value. Stakeholders describe the relationship as "they only come to us when they need something." Your own requests consistently feel like interruptions rather than welcome interactions.

**Don't confuse this with:** Transactional exchange or manipulation. Reciprocity is a social norm, not a technique — the distinction is whether the initial giving is genuine (you're providing something because it's valuable to them) or calculated (you're providing something specifically to create an obligation). The false positive: giving something and then immediately asking for something back converts reciprocity into transactional pressure; the sequencing needs space between the giving and the request for reciprocity to function as a norm rather than a trade.

**Try Noticing:** In a current working relationship where you need something — a stakeholder's time, a colleague's buy-in, a team's participation — notice whether there is a recent preceding context in which you provided something genuinely useful to them. If the relationship starts and ends with your requests, reciprocity is unavailable as a mechanism. The question is not "have I been nice to them?" but "have I given them something they actually valued?"
