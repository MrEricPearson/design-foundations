# Primary vs. Secondary Research
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Foundational for 202 arc (research mechanics); pairs with 149 and 150

**Goal:** Recognize whether data came from direct contact with the source or from someone else's documentation — because the two carry different levels of reliability and different kinds of blind spots.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that data is data — a study, a report, or a prior research synthesis all count as "we have research on this." The correction: who collected the data and when they collected it determines what the data can tell you, and secondary research carries the invisible assumptions of the original collection context. The mechanism: primary research is data you collect yourself through direct contact with the source — observing, interviewing, watching. It surfaces what is true now, in this specific context. Secondary research is data someone else collected and documented — a study, a report, a survey your company ran, a database of user complaints. It surfaces what was true when it was gathered, in whatever context shaped the original collection. Using secondary data without acknowledging those contextual constraints means making decisions based on what was true somewhere else, at some point — which may or may not generalize.

**You'll see it when:** A team makes a design decision based on "the research shows..." — and the research is a three-year-old industry report, a competitor's case study, or a colleague's recollection of a conversation from last quarter. Those are secondary sources, used as if they were primary.

**The signal:** Ask: "Who collected this data, and when did they collect it?" If the answer is "we did, recently" — that's primary. If the answer is "someone else wrote it up" or "I don't know the original source" — that's secondary. Secondary sources often hide in plain sight as "the data shows" without attribution.

**Don't confuse this with:** The question of which type of research is better. Secondary research isn't inferior — it's often the right starting point before primary research begins, and it's sometimes the only data available. The risk isn't using secondary research; it's using it without acknowledging that it was gathered in a different context and may not generalize.

**Try Noticing:** Think about the last research finding that shaped a decision in your work. Can you trace it back to a specific source? Who collected it, when, and from whom? The traceability test tells you whether you're working with primary data or a secondary layer of interpretation.
