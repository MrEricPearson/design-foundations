# Weighing Trade-offs
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 101 | **Episode:** 2

**Goal:** Recognize when a multi-option decision is being debated without the disagreement being made explicit — and externalize the judgment so what's actually in dispute becomes visible.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that the right option will become clear if you discuss it long enough. The correction: discussion without externalization produces longer debate, not clearer decisions. The mechanism: when options are held in people's heads, apparent disagreement about options is often actually disagreement about unstated beliefs about impact or effort. Two people can prefer different options not because they've evaluated the same things differently, but because they're evaluating different things — and neither can see the other's actual basis. Making impact and effort explicit doesn't settle the disagreement; it surfaces where the disagreement actually lives. That shift — from "we disagree about what to do" to "we disagree about how costly this is" — is usually much faster to resolve.

**The move:** For each option, write a quick rating: impact (high/low: how much does this matter if we get it right?) and effort (high/low: how much does this cost to do?). Do it independently before comparing. Where the ratings differ, that's what to discuss — not the options themselves.

**Don't confuse this with a formula.** The impact/effort grid is a thinking scaffold, not a decision calculator. A false positive: when everyone rates options the same way, the decision feels resolved. But identical ratings arrived at without discussion may reflect shared assumptions, not shared judgment — and the shared assumptions may be wrong. If the ratings come easily and immediately, it's worth asking what you might be assuming about impact or effort that someone else wouldn't.

**Try Noticing:** Look at a decision currently being debated. Can you name what's actually in dispute — is it impact (how much it matters) or effort (how much it costs)? Or is it something else entirely that neither option/effort framing captures?
