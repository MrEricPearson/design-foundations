# Giving and Receiving Feedback
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** none | **Episode:** 2

**Goal:** Recognize the difference between a verdict and feedback — so you can give feedback that can be acted on and receive it without collapsing into defensiveness.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that honest reactions are useful feedback — that if you share your genuine response to someone's work, they have something to work with. The correction: a reaction without specificity isn't actionable, and an unspecific reaction produces defensiveness rather than improvement. The mechanism: a verdict ("I don't love it," "this feels off," "this isn't quite right") closes the exchange. The person receiving it has only two options: accept or resist. Feedback names what specifically isn't working and why, which opens a third option — understanding, which is what produces actual change. The distinction isn't about being kind or harsh; it's about whether the feedback produces something the other person can do differently.

The same problem applies in reverse. Receiving a verdict as if it's feedback produces defensive responses because you're being asked to respond to a feeling with no point of entry. Receiving feedback by asking a clarifying question before responding (rather than explaining your decision) converts the exchange from evaluation to investigation.

**You'll see it when:** A review session ends with the receiver unsure what specifically to change. Or the reviewer leaves frustrated that their concerns weren't addressed, even though they were acknowledged.

**The signal:** The feedback exchange ends with agreement ("got it," "I'll look at it") but no specific next action that could be verified.

**Don't confuse feedback with critique.** Critique evaluates the work against a standard — it can identify whether something is working without prescribing a fix. Feedback points at what to examine more closely, which includes but extends beyond formal critique. A false positive: asking "what were you trying to accomplish here?" feels like curiosity when it's occasionally used to preemptively justify the work rather than to genuinely understand it before responding. The question is most useful when both sides enter it open to what the answer might reveal.

**Try Noticing:** In your next feedback conversation — giving or receiving — notice whether the exchange ends with a specific next action or with a general acknowledgment. If it's the latter, what specific question went unasked?
