# Research vs. Anecdote
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Foundational for 202 arc; pairs with 148 and 150

**Goal:** Recognize when a data claim is grounded in collected evidence versus drawn from a memorable story — so you can weight findings correctly when making decisions.

**Concept:** Research is data collected with intent and documentation: you planned to learn something specific, gathered observations or responses from multiple sources, and recorded what you found. An anecdote is a specific remembered instance — a support call someone took, a demo where a user struggled, a hallway conversation that stuck. Both can carry genuine insight. The difference is what they justify: anecdotes point toward a question worth investigating; research supports a conclusion. Using an anecdote as a conclusion and using research as just a prompt are both errors, in opposite directions.

**You'll see it when:** A meeting produces the phrase "I heard from a client that..." or "when I used this product I couldn't figure out..." used as the basis for a product decision. The story is real. The question is whether it represents a pattern or an instance.

**The signal:** Anecdotes have a subject: "a user," "my colleague," "one customer." Research has a method: "we interviewed eight people and found..." or "the session recordings showed that across six participants..." If the source of a claim is a person's memory of an instance, it's an anecdote. If it's a documented collection from multiple sources, it's research.

**Don't confuse this with:** Anecdotes being useless. A well-placed anecdote reveals a real experience that might not show up in quantitative data — it's why practitioners with product experience often surface problems that measurement misses. The problem is treating a one-time instance as proof of a pattern that requires action. An anecdote is a hypothesis generator, not a hypothesis test.

**Try Noticing:** In the next meeting where research findings come up, notice the phrasing. Does the claim come with a source and a collection method? Or does it come from someone's memory of a specific interaction? That distinction tells you whether the claim is ready to act on or ready to investigate.

**What Next:** When you want to investigate whether an anecdote reflects a real pattern, read 202 (Research Mechanics) for how to gather primary data that would confirm or disconfirm it.
