# Correlation vs. Causation
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 100 (Assumption vs. Fact), 135 (Leading vs. Lagging Indicators) | **Cluster:** K — Data Literacy

**Goal:** Recognize when a data point shows two things moving together — and distinguish that from evidence that one thing caused the other.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that when two metrics move at the same time — feature usage goes up while retention goes up, or support tickets go up after a release — one caused the other. The correction: co-movement (correlation) is a mathematical relationship between two variables. It says nothing about direction, nothing about mechanism, and nothing about what would happen if one were changed independently. Causation requires a mechanism: a specific, testable way that changing A produces a change in B. Correlation does not establish that mechanism, and cannot.

The mechanism: System 1 is a pattern-completion engine. When two things are observed together repeatedly, S1 treats the co-occurrence as a causal unit — A and B happen together, so A causes B or B causes A. This is a powerful heuristic for learning in a physical world where causes and effects are spatially and temporally proximate. In product analytics, where the actual causal chain between a feature change and a behavior metric may run through dozens of unmeasured variables, the same heuristic produces systematically wrong conclusions. S1 supplies the causal story; the dashboard supplies the numbers that make it look real.

Common confounds: both variables are caused by a third unmeasured variable (users who engage deeply with one feature also engage deeply with another — the third variable is "engaged users"); time (a feature launched at the same time as a seasonal peak in usage); or survivorship (the users in your analytics are the ones who stayed, and their behavior has a different baseline than all users).

**You'll see it when:** A feature change is attributed to a metric movement because the metric moved at the same time the feature shipped. A dashboard is used as evidence for a causal claim without any discussion of what would need to be true for the correlation to be causal. Two teams each claim credit for the same metric improvement because each made a change in the same period.

**The signal:** The causal claim arrived without a named mechanism. If you can't answer "specifically how does A cause B?" — what chain of behavior, decision, or psychological response connects them — the causal claim is unsupported. The test: can you run an intervention that changes A while holding everything else constant and observe whether B changes? If the intervention is impossible or untested, the causal claim is an assumption, not a finding.

**Don't confuse this with:** Useful correlations that are valuable even when causation isn't established. Correlation can be a valid input to decision-making without being causal — knowing that two things tend to co-occur is sometimes all you need. The false positive: dismissing a correlation because causation isn't proven, when the correlation is strong enough to inform a decision even without a proven mechanism. The distinction matters when you're designing an intervention: if you want to change B by changing A, you need causation. If you're only using the correlation to predict where B will go, causation isn't required.

**Try Noticing:** The next time someone presents a data finding, listen for the word "shows" or "proves" — "the data shows that feature X increases retention." Notice whether a mechanism was named or implied, or whether the causal claim was attached directly to the co-movement. A finding described as "users who use X retain at a higher rate" is different from "X increases retention." The first is a correlation. The second is a causal claim. They sound similar; they imply completely different actions.
