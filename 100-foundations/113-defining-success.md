# Defining Success Before You Start
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** None | **Wave:** 1

**Goal:** Recognize when success is undefined before work begins — and write the one sentence that makes it checkable rather than retroactively adjusted.

---

The problem with "I'll know it when I see it" is that you always do.

That's not a critique. It's a description of something your brain does automatically. The moment you know an outcome, you can't evaluate it the way you would have before it happened. Baruch Fischhoff (1975) was the first to document this in a controlled study: participants told an event's outcome consistently believed they "would have predicted it," while participants who didn't know the outcome predicted it at far lower rates. He named the mechanism "creeping determinism." Once you know how something turned out, the path to that outcome starts to feel logical. Obvious, even. Like it couldn't have gone any other way.

A success definition written after the result exists runs directly into this. You're trying to evaluate the outcome using criteria shaped by that same outcome. The criteria bend to fit, not because you're being dishonest, but because you can't see a result without your judgment of it adjusting. A definition built after the work is done will almost always validate the work. The definition becomes a receipt, not a test. And a receipt always matches the purchase.

This is why retrospectives and success reviews feel productive but land softly. There's genuine discussion. Takeaways get captured. The problem is that none of it constitutes a real evaluation if there was nothing to evaluate against. "We think it worked" is an opinion. "It hit the target we set before we started" is a finding.

The correction is one sentence. Written before you start.

---

The single move is to complete this: "I'll know this worked if ___."

The blank has to be fillable with something observable before the work is done: a behavior, an outcome, a number you can check. Not a feeling ("it seems smoother"), a deliverable ("we shipped it"), or a milestone ("we finished the phase"). Those describe the work. A success definition describes what you'll use to know the work did what it was supposed to do.

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham spent four decades studying what happens when goals are specific versus vague. Their review of roughly 400 laboratory and field studies found that specific, observable goals outperformed "do your best" targets about 90% of the time (Locke & Latham, 2002). The specificity isn't just motivating. It's what makes evaluation possible afterward. You can't measure against a feeling. You can't be wrong about a milestone. You can be wrong about whether activation improved. That wrongness is the feature.

A definition written before work begins can be wrong. That's the entire point.

Gary Klein formalized a related discipline in his Harvard Business Review article on pre-mortems (Klein, 2007), grounded in earlier research by Mitchell, Russo, and Pennington (1989). Their studies found that prospective hindsight, placing yourself mentally in the future and looking back, increases the accuracy of identifying what will matter by 30%. The pre-mortem asks "what would failure look like?" before work starts. Writing "I'll know this worked if ___" asks the opposite question first: what would success actually look like? Both work for the same reason. You're committing to a standard before the result is visible, which is the only moment the standard can be honest.

Write the sentence. Put it somewhere you'll see it. That's the whole method.

---

The confusion that breaks this most often is between success and scope.

Scope describes what you'll build. Success describes what you'll use to know if building it worked. Both are useful. Only one can be found wrong after the fact.

"Ship the redesigned onboarding flow" is scope. "I'll know this worked if new user activation improves in the first session" is success. A project can clear every item on the scope list and still not succeed, and it can miss scope targets and still succeed if the thing that actually mattered changed. Scope and success point in different directions. It's worth knowing which one you're tracking before the work starts, not after.

The false positive to watch for: a detailed project plan with clear deliverables feels like a success definition. It isn't. Deliverables describe what gets built. The success definition describes what you'll find out.

(And if you've watched a team declare victory because everything shipped on time while the numbers stayed flat — you've seen exactly what happens when scope gets promoted to success.)

---

Look at something you're currently working on. Not something finished. Something active right now.

Can you complete "I'll know this worked if ___" in a way that could be checked, and be found false? That last part matters. Run the blank test: if what fills it is a feeling, a feature, or a milestone, write it again. Falsifiability is what you're after. A success definition that can't be wrong isn't a definition. It's just documentation of what you're hoping for.

This doesn't have to be precise. "Improves" is vague; "improves by 10%" is checkable. But even a rough observable standard, something tied to behavior rather than delivery, is more honest than nothing. You're not designing a measurement system. You're writing one sentence that makes the retrospective a real evaluation rather than a reconstruction.

One pass is enough. Do it before the next phase starts.
