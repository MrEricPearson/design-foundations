# Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 101, 103 | **Episode:** 3

**Goal:** Recognize when design evidence is based on what people said versus what people did, so you can correctly interpret what the evidence actually proves.

**Concept:** The working assumption in most practitioner teams is that user research produces facts about users. The correction: user research produces two categorically different kinds of data, and treating them as interchangeable is the source of a specific class of design failure — solutions to problems users described but don't actually have, in the form they described.

What people say in interviews, surveys, and usability debriefs is self-report: a verbal account of experience, preference, or anticipated behavior. What people do during a task, in a real context, over time, is observed behavior. These diverge — not randomly, not occasionally, but predictably and systematically.

The mechanism: self-report is reconstructed memory filtered through social expectation and motivated reasoning. When someone is asked what they do or what they want, they produce a description of what they believe they do, or what they think the interviewer expects, or what they aspire to do in an idealized version of the situation. This is not dishonesty — it is how memory and self-presentation work. The gap between stated and actual behavior is a feature of human cognition that applies universally.

Self-report is excellent evidence for what it actually measures: context, motivation, mental models, retrospective experience, and emotional states. It is unreliable evidence for what people will do in a real situation under real constraints.

**You'll see it when:** A team cites interview findings as evidence for a behavior — "we talked to twelve people and they all said they want X" — and builds a design around it without observation data to corroborate what they actually do.

**The signal:** The evidence for a design decision references what users said rather than what users did during a task, in a real context, or measured over time.

**Don't confuse this with:** The idea that interviews are weak research. Interviews generate access to things that cannot be observed — motivation, prior experience, mental models. The problem is not the method; it is applying self-report data to questions about behavior. A false positive: a team runs moderated sessions and collects thick notes — this feels like rigorous evidence, and it is, but only for the questions self-report can answer. If those notes are then used to make behavioral predictions, the rigor of the research process doesn't transfer to the reliability of the behavioral claim.

**Try Noticing:** Review the last three significant design decisions in your current work. For each: is the supporting evidence self-report ("users told us") or observed behavior ("we watched users do")? If all three are self-report, that is not automatically wrong — but it means you have untested behavioral assumptions. Identify which claims would change if observation data contradicted them.
