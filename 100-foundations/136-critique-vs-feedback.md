# Critique vs. Feedback
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 115 | **Episode:** 14

**Goal:** Recognize which mode a review session is operating in — evaluative or observational — so you can set the right expectation and get the type of input the work actually needs.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that "getting feedback" and "getting a critique" are the same thing — you show the work, people respond, and you take notes. The correction: critique and feedback are structurally different modes that require different setups and produce different outputs — and mixing them without naming the switch produces defensiveness, approval-seeking, or both. The mechanism: critique evaluates work against a stated standard. The question is "does this accomplish [specific goal]?" and the output is a gap list. Critique requires evaluators who know the goal and the context. Feedback is observational input about experience. The question is "what do you notice?" and the output is experience data. Feedback requires observers who are relatively naive to the design intent. Mixing them without naming the switch puts participants in two incompatible roles simultaneously — they're trying to evaluate (which requires knowing the standard) and observe (which requires not having a predetermined standard).

The fix is structural: declare the mode before the session starts. One sentence. Don't mix modes without naming the transition.

**You'll see it when:** A review produces defensiveness (critique arrived when the person expected feedback — they felt evaluated rather than heard). Or a review produces vague reactions without actionable findings (feedback arrived when critique was needed — observations without standards don't produce a gap list). Or the same session drifts between modes without anyone naming it.

**The signal:** The session ends and it's unclear whether the output is a list of specific problems to address or a collection of observations to consider. Both are useful. Neither in ambiguous form is actionable.

**Don't confuse critique with criticism.** Criticism is a negative judgment about quality. Critique is structured evaluation of whether work accomplishes a stated goal — it can conclude that something works. A false positive: a session where everyone nods and says "this looks great" feels like positive feedback. But if the session was set up as critique (with a stated goal), unanimous positive response is either genuine validation or social approval-seeking — and you can't tell which without a stated goal against which "great" could mean something specific.

**Try Noticing:** Think of your last review session. Before it started, was the mode declared — evaluative or observational? What did the output look like — a gap list or a collection of reactions? Did the setup match what was needed?
