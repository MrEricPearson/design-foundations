# Attachment Is the Real Risk
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** The Risk in What You Assume, Part 4 | **Prereqs:** 102 | **Episode:** 1

**Goal:** Recognize the difference between confidence and attachment — so you can identify when a direction is being held because it's been validated versus because it's been invested in.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that the risk in early work is choosing the wrong starting point — beginning with code instead of a prototype, building instead of sketching. The correction: starting point doesn't determine risk; attachment does. The mechanism: a direction becomes costly to change not when it's wrong, but when the person holding it has shifted from testing a hypothesis to defending an investment. That shift is invisible from the outside because confidence and attachment produce the same visible behavior — both defend the direction, both dismiss contradictory evidence. The distinction is internal: confidence can name the conditions under which it would change course; attachment cannot.

Every hour spent on a direction that's now "mine" increases the psychological cost of acknowledging it's wrong. The risk compound as time passes — not because the direction becomes more likely to be wrong, but because changing it becomes harder to justify.

**You'll see it when:** Evidence that contradicts a direction gets contextualized ("that's an edge case"), minimized ("users always say that in research but they behave differently"), or deferred ("we'll address that in the next phase"). Sunk cost reasoning appears: "We've already built this — we can't just throw it away."

**The signal:** When asked "what would have to be true for this direction to be wrong?", the response is "I don't think it is wrong" rather than a named condition. Confidence can answer the question; attachment deflects it.

**Don't confuse attachment with confidence.** Strong conviction in a validated direction looks identical to attachment from the outside. A false positive: a team that has tested a direction and found consistent support defends it the same way an attached team defends an untested one — with conviction. The distinguishing test is not how strongly they hold the view but whether they can articulate falsifiability: what would they need to see to change course? A team with genuine confidence names conditions. An attached team argues there are none.

**Try Noticing:** Name one direction you're currently building toward. Ask: if you saw solid evidence it was wrong, what would you actually do? Notice whether your honest answer is "change course" or "look for reasons the evidence is flawed."
