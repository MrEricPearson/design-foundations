# Nielsen's 10 Heuristics
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 123 | **Episode:** 12

**Goal:** Recognize when a usability problem you're observing matches a named category — so you can articulate it specifically rather than defending a vague reaction.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that usability problems are found by asking "does this feel right?" — a judgment that's difficult to communicate, defend, or act on without shared vocabulary. The correction: usability problems cluster into ten recurring categories, and naming which category a problem belongs to transforms a personal reaction ("this feels wrong") into a specific, arguable observation ("this violates heuristic 3 — there's no way to undo the action"). The mechanism: a named category can be communicated, prioritized, compared, and fixed. A vague reaction can only be accepted or resisted.

The ten:
1. **Visibility of system status** — users always know what's happening
2. **Match between system and real world** — language and concepts match what users already know
3. **User control and freedom** — users can undo and exit unintended states
4. **Consistency and standards** — same things look and behave the same throughout
5. **Error prevention** — the design prevents mistakes before they occur
6. **Recognition over recall** — users see options rather than remembering them
7. **Flexibility and efficiency** — experts can use shortcuts; novices have a clear path
8. **Aesthetic and minimalist design** — every element earns its place; irrelevant content competes with relevant
9. **Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors** — error messages are plain, specific, and constructive
10. **Help and documentation** — if needed, it's findable and task-focused

**You'll see it when:** A review session produces "I don't like this" or "something feels off" without anyone being able to name what specifically is failing. Or when a usability problem is dismissed as a matter of taste when it's actually a structural violation.

**The signal:** A reaction to an interface element that can't be articulated beyond feeling wrong. The heuristics are vocabulary for translating that feeling into a named category.

**Don't confuse heuristics with user testing.** Heuristics surface what expert evaluators can find by applying a framework to an interface; usability testing surfaces what experts miss because they know how the system is supposed to work. Both catch different problems — heuristic review catches structural violations; usability sessions catch behavioral patterns. A false positive: a clean heuristic evaluation that surfaces no violations feels like a product that users can operate. Expert evaluators applying heuristics still miss problems that only surface when an unfamiliar person attempts a real task.

**Try Noticing:** Look at one screen in your current product. Can you find one element that produces a reaction ("this doesn't quite work") without a specific explanation? Now run it against the 10 categories: which heuristic does it violate, and specifically how?
