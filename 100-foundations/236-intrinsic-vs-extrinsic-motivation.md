# Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 101, 125 | **Episode:** 4

**Goal:** Recognize which type of motivation is driving a user behavior, because the two respond to different design interventions and can undermine each other when confused.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that motivation is a single dial — users are either motivated to do something or they aren't, and more incentives increase motivation. The correction: intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are categorically different states that respond to different conditions, and introducing extrinsic incentives into an intrinsically motivated behavior can reduce motivation overall.

The mechanism: intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself — a user does something because they find it meaningful, satisfying, or inherently valuable. Extrinsic motivation comes from a consequence of the activity — a user does something to receive a reward or avoid a penalty. Both produce behavior, but under very different conditions and with very different fragility.

The crowding-out effect (overjustification): when an extrinsic reward is introduced for an intrinsically motivated behavior, the brain shifts its causal model. "I do this because I value it" becomes "I do this for the reward." When the reward is removed, the original intrinsic motivation doesn't return — it has been displaced. Studies on children and adults show consistent patterns: pay someone to do something they were doing for free and they'll stop when you stop paying.

For design, this matters in two situations. First, when adding rewards or incentives to existing behavior: will the incentive crowd out motivation that's already there? Second, when designing new habits: the long-term stability of the behavior depends on building genuine intrinsic motivation, not on maintaining extrinsic reward delivery.

Self-determination theory identifies three conditions that cultivate intrinsic motivation: autonomy (the user feels they're choosing the activity), competence (the user experiences themselves as getting better at something meaningful), and relatedness (the activity connects them to others or to something larger).

**You'll see it when:** Users who were engaged with a product show reduced engagement after a reward program ends. Or when badge and streak removal produces more disruption than the product's core value loss would explain. The users had been working for the rewards, not for the underlying goal.

**The signal:** Users describe their relationship to a product primarily in terms of what they'll earn or lose from it, rather than what they get from the activity itself.

**Don't confuse this with:** Extrinsic motivation being inherently inferior. For behaviors with low inherent appeal — administrative tasks, compliance actions, maintenance activities — extrinsic motivation is often the right design target. The question is whether intrinsic motivation exists or could be cultivated, because if it can, extrinsic rewards are risky additions.

**Try Noticing:** Think about a feature or behavior your product is trying to encourage. Ask: is there a version of this behavior the user would genuinely want to do if it were easier, more meaningful, or socially connected — independent of any reward? Or is the behavior inherently low-appeal, where the design goal is to make the reward worth the effort? The answer shapes whether you're designing for intrinsic motivation cultivation or extrinsic incentive structure.
