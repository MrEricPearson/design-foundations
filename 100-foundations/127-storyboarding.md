# Storyboarding
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 106 | **Episode:** 2

**Goal:** Recognize when a proposed flow needs to be communicated as a sequence before any screen design exists — and make the logic of that sequence visible without committing to details.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that a flow can be communicated through description or screen mocks — either explain it verbally or show what the screens will look like. The correction: a described flow produces reactions to the description; a mocked screen produces reactions to the visual design. Neither produces reactions to the logic of the sequence — whether the before/during/after actually makes sense for the person experiencing it. The mechanism: sequence logic operates at a level between "what are we building" and "what will it look like." A storyboard makes that level visible as a series of moments: what triggers the need, what happens at each key interaction point, and what the person ends up with. Problems at this level — a step that happens before the user has enough context, an outcome that doesn't follow from the interaction, a transition that skips a necessary decision — are nearly invisible in screens and often surfaced by the sequence itself.

**The move:** Draw 3–6 panels showing the trigger, key interaction moments, and outcome for one user flow. Stick figures, boxes, and captions are the standard. Label each panel with what's happening and why it matters. The last panel shows the user in a changed state — what's different for them.

**Don't confuse a storyboard with a wireframe.** A storyboard shows the sequence of a user's experience — before, during, after. A wireframe shows a single screen in layout detail. Sequence comes before layout — a wireframe is the answer to a question the storyboard hasn't yet confirmed makes sense. A false positive: a well-drawn storyboard where every panel is detailed and aesthetically consistent looks professional and communicates confidence in the direction. But rough storyboards invite feedback on logic; polished ones read as settled decisions. Rough is better until the sequence is validated.

**Try Noticing:** Think of a user flow you're currently working on. Can you represent it as a before/during/after without drawing any screen details — just the moments and what changes between them? Notice where you're uncertain about what goes in a panel: that uncertainty marks the part of the logic that isn't yet resolved.

**What Next:** To move from sequence to testable flow, read 132 (Prototype Fidelity) — it helps you decide how much to build vs. leave rough. To connect the storyboard to a real user context before drawing it, read 130 (Scenario Writing) first.
