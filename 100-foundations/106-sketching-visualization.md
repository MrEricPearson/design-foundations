# Sketching as Externalization
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** none | **Episode:** 2

**Goal:** Recognize when an idea needs to be externalized — drawn, not described — so it can be reacted to rather than imagined.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that an idea needs to be polished or well-developed before it can be shared. The correction: a sketch doesn't communicate an idea; it creates a shared object that can be pointed at, questioned, and marked up. The mechanism: a verbal description of an idea produces reactions to your framing of it — the listener hears your interpretation, not the idea. A sketch produces reactions to the sketch itself — the viewer's interpretation, which is usually different and more valuable. That difference is the whole point. An idea that only exists in prose or speech can't be questioned without questioning the speaker. An idea that exists as a rough drawing can be questioned, crossed out, and redrawn without anyone losing face.

**The move:** Draw it — badly is fine. Boxes, arrows, labels, a rough sequence. Aim for two minutes. The measure of success is whether someone else can point at something in it and react to it specifically.

**Don't confuse sketching with designing.** Design decisions come after the idea is visible and has been reacted to. A sketch is a question, not an answer. A false positive: a clean, well-organized diagram communicates confidence in a direction — but it also makes it harder for others to suggest that the direction itself might be wrong. Rough sketches invite disagreement; polished ones discourage it.

**Try Noticing:** Think of an idea currently living only in your head or in paragraph form. What would you draw if you had to represent it with boxes and arrows in two minutes? Notice where you hesitate — that hesitation often marks the part of the idea that's least resolved.
