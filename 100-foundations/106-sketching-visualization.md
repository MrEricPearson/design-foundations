# The Idea in Your Head That Nobody Can Push Back On
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** none | **Episode:** 2

*When an idea only exists in your words, pushing back on it means pushing back on you.*

---

You describe an idea in a meeting. A feature, a flow, a handoff between two systems. You're clear. People follow along. Someone asks a question; you answer it. More nodding. The meeting ends.

Nothing got pushed back on.

That's not a sign the idea was good. It's a sign the idea had nowhere to land.

---

There's an assumption most people carry into early conversations about an idea: it needs to be further along before it can be shared. More developed, more organized, more clearly thought through. The correction is smaller than that. The idea doesn't need to be further along. It needs to be visible.

When something only exists in your words, other people can't react to the idea. They can only react to your framing of it. They hear your sequencing, your emphasis, the way you've decided to describe it. Any real disagreement — the kind where someone says "wait, does that work if X happens?" — becomes an argument with the speaker, not a question about the concept. Most people won't start that argument, especially early, especially when you clearly know more about it than they do. So they nod.

A sketch changes this completely. Even a rough one creates a shared object: something both of you can look at, point at, and react to without it involving the person who made it. Donald Schön (1983) called this a "conversation with the situation." When a designer draws rather than describes, the drawing starts to talk back. Implications appear that weren't visible in the verbal version. Gaps show up. The sketch reveals what the description was holding together by sheer confidence.

Suwa and Tversky (1997) documented what this looks like when people actually do it. Experienced designers don't just externalize ideas through sketching — they discover them. Things that felt resolved while the idea was inside your head turn out to be open questions the moment you try to put them on paper. The part of the flow you were describing smoothly? When you draw it, you find you don't know what to put in the second box. That hesitation is information. You got it from drawing, not from talking.

That's what externalization means. Not showing people what you've already figured out. Making it possible to figure out what you haven't.

---

The move is one step: draw it.

Badly is fine. Open a drawing tool, grab a whiteboard, flip to a blank page in your notebook. Draw boxes for the main pieces. Draw arrows showing what flows from what. Label each box — not beautifully, just enough that someone could point to one and ask a question about it. If there's a sequence, show the sequence. Two minutes is enough. The measure of success isn't how it looks. It's whether someone can point at something specific and react to it.

If someone points at a box and says "wait, what goes in there — is that the request or the response?" — it worked. You have something now that you didn't have from the description alone.

---

Sketching is not designing. They're different moves at different moments.

A sketch is a question. Design decisions come after the idea is visible and has been reacted to. The sketch is the step before that.

Here's where people get tripped up: a clean, organized diagram signals the opposite of what you want. Bill Buxton (2007) made this case directly — rough and polished aren't just aesthetic differences. They're communication differences. When something looks finished, people read it as finished. They stop asking whether the direction is right and start suggesting which color the boxes should be. That's not what early feedback is for. (And yes, this is exactly what happens when someone opens Figma before the conversation instead of during it.)

Rough invites disagreement. Polished discourages it. The rougher the sketch, the more clearly it communicates: this is still open, come at the idea itself.

A polished diagram can move through a meeting unchallenged not because it was right, but because it looked decided.

---

Think about something you're working on right now — a feature you've been describing in conversations, a proposal you've written out in paragraph form, a direction you've been arguing for in reviews. Pick the one where you're not completely sure other people are tracking it the way you mean it.

What would you draw if you had two minutes and a blank page? Boxes for the main pieces. Arrows for what flows from what. A rough sequence. You don't need to know how to draw. You need to name the things and show how they connect.

Notice where you hesitate. That's not a drawing problem. The hesitation marks the parts of the idea that aren't settled yet. The places where you're not sure what the arrow should point to, or what to call the box, or how many steps there are — those are the open questions your description was papering over. The sketch doesn't just show other people the idea. It shows you which parts aren't resolved.

That's the whole move. Draw it, notice what you can't draw, and watch what other people point at.
