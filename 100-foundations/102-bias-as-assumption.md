# Bias as Assumption
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** The Risk in What You Assume, Part 3 | **Prereqs:** 101 | **Episode:** 1

**Goal:** Recognize that some assumptions can't be surfaced by introspection alone — and identify the structural move that surfaces them anyway.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that assumptions are things you've thought explicitly — if you haven't stated it as an assumption, it isn't one. The correction: bias is the subclass of assumption that bypassed explicit formulation entirely. It operates below the threshold of "I'm assuming" because it arrived as background context, professional experience, or social consensus rather than as a reasoned claim. The mechanism that makes it hard to surface: the more familiar an assumption is, the more it resembles observation. You don't notice yourself assuming "users will want to do this through the interface" when you've always worked on interface-centered products. The assumption isn't invisible because you're not looking — it's invisible because familiarity has made it indistinguishable from fact.

External perspectives don't share your familiarity. What appears as context to you appears as assumption to someone who walked in fresh.

**The move:** Ask someone genuinely outside your current work — not just outside the immediate task, but outside the framing — one question: "What am I assuming here that you wouldn't assume?" Write down the first answer without explaining why it isn't a problem.

**Don't confuse naming a bias with resolving it.** Spotting "that's confirmation bias" is a label on the assumption, not a response to it. The same assumption with a name is still an assumption — now visible, but still unresolved. A false positive: getting pushback from a colleague feels like you've pressure-tested your assumptions. If your colleague shares your context (same team, same codebase, same framing), their objections arise from shared assumptions — the blind spot you're trying to surface is the one you both have.

**Try Noticing:** Think of a direction in your current work that would be hard to abandon. What does someone new to this project see that you no longer notice? What framing, priority, or constraint "everyone knows" that a fresh person would question?

**What Next:** If you've named the bias but the direction still feels difficult to question, read 103 (Attachment Is the Real Risk) — that's the layer underneath, where the cognitive problem becomes an emotional one.
