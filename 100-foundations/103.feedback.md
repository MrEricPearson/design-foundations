# Peer Review Feedback — 103: Attachment Is the Real Risk

## Reviewer 1 (anonymous, UX peer, non-native English speaker)
**Date:** 2026-08-19 | **Status:** Collected, not yet acted on

### Raw feedback (paraphrased from conversation)
- Hard to connect with the Staw paragraph ("Barry Staw studied this in 1976...")
- Found the IKEA Effect conclusion important and well-placed, but the academic presentation style was a barrier
- Specifically: the investment/management simulation framing felt distant — "too academic"
- Understood the content but got lost / wanted to skip the Staw section
- The Ross, Lepper, and Hubbard paragraph did NOT have the same problem — "you caught my attention talking to ME"
- What worked: the "you" address, the direct talk-to-the-reader voice throughout the rest of the piece
- Language note: reviewer is non-native English speaker — may amplify comprehension issues where they exist

### Analysis / hypothesis
The Staw section fails because it drops the reader from second-person ("you") into third-person (business students in a management simulation). The experiment's setup is abstract and foreign — nobody has been a business student making investment decisions in a corporate simulation. No experiential bridge exists before or after.

The IKEA section didn't fail for the same reason because:
1. The setup is universally relatable (everyone has assembled something)
2. The BILLY bookcase parenthetical explicitly bridges the experiment to the reader's personal experience

Ross/Lepper didn't fail because the paragraph stayed close to the reader's world and returned to second person quickly.

**Root cause:** Abstract experiments need an experiential bridge — before (anchor the reader in a parallel personal situation) or after (explicitly translate the experiment to the reader's world). Staw had neither. The experiment is proof, not the point. The reader's experience is the point.

### Proposed fix (pending additional reviewer feedback)
Wrap the Staw section with an experiential bridge. Either:
- Before: briefly anchor in a recognizable personal moment before introducing the study
- After: explicitly translate — "You're not a business student, but you've run this experiment. Every time you've kept defending something because you started it..."
- Or both

Do not remove Staw — the finding is load-bearing. Change how it's introduced and closed.

### Rule to add to spec
Every piece of research needs an experiential bridge to the reader's world, before or after the study is described. Readers need to see themselves in the evidence, not just understand it.
