# Behavior vs. Attitude
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Foundational prereq for 202 (Research Mechanics), 214 (Affinity Mapping), and 301 (From Vague Ask to Real Persona); pairs with 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior)

**Goal:** Recognize when research is capturing attitude (what people think or feel) versus behavior (what people do) — because the decision it's being used to make may need one and not the other.

**Concept:** Attitude data tells you what people think, believe, prefer, or feel. Behavioral data tells you what people do. These frequently diverge: high satisfaction scores don't always predict continued use, and low expressed frustration doesn't always mean a task is easy. Choosing the right research approach means identifying which type of data the decision actually requires.

**You'll see it when:** Research findings are being used to predict behavior, but the data is entirely attitudinal. "Users said they find it easy" being used to conclude "users won't struggle with this" is a scope mismatch — attitudinal data can't predict behavioral accuracy. Or the reverse: a decision about user experience quality is being supported by task completion rates alone, without any data on how the experience felt.

**The signal:** The decision the research is informing is behavioral ("will users complete this?") but the evidence is attitudinal ("users said they prefer this"). Or: the decision is about satisfaction or experience quality (attitude) but the evidence is entirely behavioral (click rates, completion rates, time-on-task). The data type and the decision type aren't the same.

**Don't confuse this with:** The self-report/observed distinction (151), which is about the method. Behavior vs. attitude is about the content of what's being measured. You can observe attitude (a participant's visible frustration), and you can self-report behavior ("I usually click the back button when I get stuck"). The two dimensions are related but separate.

**Try Noticing:** For a decision you're currently involved in, ask: is the decision behavioral or attitudinal? Then check: is the evidence behavioral or attitudinal? If they don't match, the research may be answering a different question than the decision requires.

**What Next:** Read 202 (Research Mechanics) for approaches that generate behavioral versus attitudinal data — and the access conditions that differ between them. The conversational, observational, and async approaches each generate different mixes of the two data types.
