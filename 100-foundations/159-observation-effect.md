# Observation Effect
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Soft prereq for 215a (Moderated Usability Session); pairs with 157 (Why You Don't Help During Testing)

**Goal:** Recognize when participant behavior in a research or testing session is being shaped by the presence of an observer — so you know how to read what you're seeing.

**Concept:** When people know they're being watched, they often behave differently than they would alone: more carefully, more deliberately, more politely, or in ways they believe the observer wants to see. In a usability session, a participant who knows they're being evaluated may work harder to succeed, apologize for struggling, or attribute problems to themselves rather than the interface. In a research interview, a participant aware of organizational dynamics may soften their real frustrations. Neither behavior is dishonest — it's a natural social response to being observed. It just means what you're seeing may not reflect unobserved behavior.

**You'll see it when:** A participant in a session is much more careful, deliberate, or successful than users appear to be in real-world usage data. Or: interview participants describe an experience as mostly fine, but support tickets and complaints tell a different story. The gap between what you see in a session and what happens in production is often partly the observation effect.

**The signal:** Watch for participants who explain or apologize while working: "I'm not usually this slow," "normally I know where this is," "I think I'm doing this wrong." Those are markers that the person is managing how they appear to you — which means they're partly performing for you rather than behaving naturally.

**Don't confuse this with:** Participants being untrustworthy or the data being useless. Observed behavior still reveals real things about interface clarity, task comprehension, and error recovery — even if it's slightly performance-influenced. The observation effect doesn't invalidate session data; it's a lens for interpreting it. Data from sessions where participants struggled is real data, even if it understates typical production behavior.

**Try Noticing:** In the next usability session you observe, listen for participant self-commentary — any statement that seems directed at the observer rather than at the task. Count how many times participants explain their behavior to you rather than just doing it. That ratio tells you how much cognitive bandwidth is going into impression management versus task completion.

**What Next:** For techniques that reduce the observation effect in live sessions, read 215a (Moderated Usability Session), particularly the facilitator's role in creating a non-evaluative environment. For testing approaches where the observation effect is lower because no facilitator is present, read 215b (Unmoderated Usability Testing).
