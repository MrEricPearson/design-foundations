# When Being Watched Changes What You See

**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Soft prereq for 215a (Moderated Usability Session); pairs with 157 (Why You Don't Help During Testing)

**Goal:** Recognize when participant behavior in a session is being shaped by the observer's presence — so you can read what you're seeing accurately, not just literally.

---

The participant pauses at a navigation label for five full seconds. They hover their cursor, click something wrong, backtrack, and try again. Then they look up at you and say: "Sorry, I'm not usually this slow."

You've heard some version of that sentence in nearly every session. Stop on it.

The participant isn't just thinking about the task. They're thinking about how they look to you.

---

Erving Goffman (1959) spent his career documenting a basic fact of social life: people behave differently when an audience is present. He called it impression management — the frontstage performance we put on for others, which is genuinely different from the backstage behavior that happens when nobody's watching. This isn't deception. It's just how humans function in social situations. When you're aware of being observed, you bring a version of yourself that's more careful, more deliberate, and more intent on not looking incompetent.

This phenomenon gets a specific name in research: the Hawthorne effect, tracing back to workplace productivity studies in the 1920s. A 2022 systematic review by Berkhout et al. in *Frontiers in Medicine* quantified it across 15 studies in clinical primary care settings: the odds ratio for measurable behavior change from observation was 1.41. That's a real, reliable difference. People who know they're being watched do things differently than people who don't.

The effect isn't one thing. Bispo Júnior (2022), reviewing social desirability bias in research, identifies two layers working at the same time. The first is deliberate impression management, where someone consciously performs for the observer. The second is subtler: self-deception, where the performance becomes convincing even to the person doing it. In a session, both can run simultaneously. The participant who works unusually hard to solve a confusing flow is partly trying to look capable. The same participant who describes the product as "mostly fine" in a debrief might be starting to believe it, because performing ease can start to feel like ease.

---

You'll see the gap most clearly when session data and production data don't match. Participants complete tasks in sessions that real users regularly fail at or abandon in production. They try harder and push through confusion they'd abandon alone. Giving up in front of someone feels bad, so they don't. They read labels more carefully, second-guess themselves less quickly, and attempt flows a second time when they'd have quit after the first in normal use.

Interview data shows it too. Ask someone how their experience with a tool has been, and the presence of an interviewer pulls the answer toward the positive. Separately gathered data often tells a different story: support tickets, complaint threads, session recordings without a facilitator present. The discrepancy isn't dishonesty. It's the observation context doing what observation contexts do.

---

The specific signal to watch for is participant commentary directed at you rather than at the task.

"I'm not usually this slow." "I think I'm doing this wrong." "Normally I'd know where this is." "Sorry, this is probably me."

Each of those sentences is real-time impression management. The participant is narrating their performance for your benefit, explaining themselves, softening the evidence of struggle. Sonderegger & Sauer (2009), studying participants using mobile prototypes with observers in the room, found measurably lower task completion rates, longer task times, and increased physiological stress compared to less-observed conditions. The effect isn't subtle. It shows up in the body.

The ratio matters: participant statements directed at the task versus statements directed at you. When someone's running commentary is about what they're doing, they're working. When it's about how they're coming across, they're managing. Both can happen in the same session, often in the same minute. (And if you've ever watched a participant apologize repeatedly for not immediately finding a button that literally nobody finds, you've seen both layers running at once.)

---

None of this means sessions are unreliable. It means they're calibrated.

Observation effect doesn't produce false positives. It suppresses them. When someone who knows they're being evaluated still can't complete a task after genuine effort, that's a strong finding, probably understated relative to what happens in production. When someone struggles visibly while calling it "not a big deal," weight the behavior over the words. The observation effect bends self-report toward positive. It doesn't bend behavior by anywhere near as much.

What changes is how you read the evidence. Observed struggle is real struggle, likely more common outside the session than what you saw. High session completion rates may be slightly inflated by the extra effort participants put in when someone's watching. Verbal reports in debriefs pull toward positive. None of this invalidates the session. It tells you which signals to weight and in which direction.

---

In the next session you observe, track the self-commentary. Count statements directed at you versus statements directed at the task. By the end of the session, you'll have a rough read on how much impression management was running alongside the task work. That ratio is its own finding, woven into your research questions rather than separate from them.

**Sources:**

Berkhout, C., De Maeseneer, J., Morreel, S., & Remmen, R. (2022). Defining and evaluating the Hawthorne effect in primary care, a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Frontiers in Medicine, 9*, 977677. Meta-analysis of 15 quantified studies; odds ratio of 1.41 for behavior modification from observation; the effect involves social desirability, conformity, and observation bias, and diminishes in rigorously designed studies.

Bispo Júnior, J.P. (2022). Social desirability bias in qualitative health research. *Revista de Saúde Pública, 56*, 107. Identifies two overlapping mechanisms of behavior modification when observed: deliberate impression management and unconscious self-deception; identifies bystander presence as a primary determinant of bias magnitude.

Goffman, E. (1959). *The presentation of self in everyday life.* Doubleday. Foundational dramaturgical model of social behavior; distinguishes frontstage (observed, performance-modified) behavior from backstage (unobserved, natural) behavior; establishes impression management as a core structural feature of social interaction.

Sauro, J. (2017, November 15). *Do observers affect usability test results?* MeasuringU. Reviews usability-specific research including Sonderegger & Sauer (2009), who found that additional observer presence during mobile prototype testing produced lower task completion rates, longer task times, and measurable physiological stress compared to less-observed conditions.
