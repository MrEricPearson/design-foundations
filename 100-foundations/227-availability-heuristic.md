# Availability Heuristic
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 101, 226 | **Episode:** 3

**Goal:** Recognize when decisions are being shaped by how easily examples come to mind rather than by how frequently or representatively those examples occur.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that the examples that come to mind readily are relevant and representative. The correction: the examples that come to mind readily are the most emotionally resonant, most recently encountered, and most frequently discussed — which is categorically different from most representative.

The mechanism: the brain estimates frequency and probability by asking "how easily can I generate examples of this?" The easier examples arrive, the more common or important the brain treats the thing as being. This heuristic is usually fast and accurate enough — but it fails in the specific situations where designers most need accurate frequency estimates: choosing which edge cases matter, estimating how prevalent a user problem is, recalling what research actually showed versus what was memorable about it.

Recent events feel more frequent than older ones. Vivid or emotionally charged events feel more likely than quiet ones. Problems that have been discussed repeatedly in team meetings feel more representative than problems encountered once in a research session. In each case, memorability is doing the work that representativeness should be doing.

**You'll see it when:** A team prioritizes a feature because it comes up frequently in team discussions, without checking whether the discussion frequency corresponds to actual user frequency. Or when a single memorable research participant disproportionately influences design decisions relative to five other participants whose sessions were less dramatic.

**The signal:** The justification for prioritizing a problem references how often it comes up in conversation or how vivid the examples are, rather than measured frequency, severity, or research sample representativeness.

**Don't confuse this with:** Frequency data being wrong because it's recalled rather than measured. The heuristic produces unreliable frequency estimates — it doesn't guarantee those estimates are wrong. Measured frequency (usage data, survey counts, ticket volumes) corrects for availability bias; recalled frequency doesn't. The false positive: a team conducts research and then in synthesis the most-cited examples are the most vivid ones from the most recent sessions. The synthesis looks balanced because multiple participants are cited — but the selection of which moments were memorable enough to end up in notes skewed toward availability, not toward representativeness.

**Try Noticing:** In your current project, what is the most frequently discussed user problem? Now check: is the frequency of discussion proportional to its frequency of occurrence for actual users, or is it driven by how vivid or recent specific examples are? Identify one problem you encountered in research that has not come up in team discussions since. What made it less memorable?
