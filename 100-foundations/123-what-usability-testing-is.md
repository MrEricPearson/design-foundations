# What Usability Testing Is
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** none | **Episode:** 12

**Goal:** Recognize the difference between approval-seeking and usability testing — so you can name which one you're running and choose the format that actually answers the question you're asking.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that showing your work to people and getting their reactions constitutes testing it. The correction: a demo where people say they like the work tells you the work was approved; it tells you nothing about whether anyone can actually use it. The mechanism: approval-seeking and usability testing answer structurally different questions through structurally different processes. Approval-seeking asks stakeholders to evaluate a direction — the implicit question is "does this look right to you?" The audience knows the product, knows the intent, and is positioned to compare this version to the alternative. Usability testing asks an unfamiliar person to attempt a real task without coaching — the implicit question is "can someone complete this without help?" The participant doesn't know how it's supposed to work; they only know what they're trying to do.

The gap between the two is not about rigor or formality. It's about what evidence is produced. Five stakeholders who nod at a demo have confirmed that the team's direction is acceptable to stakeholders — a useful social confirmation, but orthogonal to whether users can complete a task.

**You'll see it when:** A review session is called "user testing" but no task completion is observed. Participants are shown a prototype and asked "what do you think?" rather than given a goal and observed attempting to achieve it. Feedback is about aesthetics and preferences rather than task completion success or failure.

**The signal:** The session produces reactions to the design ("I like this," "this feels cleaner") rather than observations of behavior ("the user paused here and looked for something that wasn't present").

**Don't confuse usability testing with research in general.** Research includes many methods — interviews, surveys, analytics review — that produce legitimate evidence about different questions. Usability testing specifically produces evidence about task completion by unfamiliar users. A false positive: showing the design to a colleague who tries to "use" it while you watch feels like usability testing. If the colleague knows the product's intent, domain, and your reasoning, their session produces expert review, not naive user behavior. The mechanism that makes usability testing valid is genuine unfamiliarity with the intent.

**Try Noticing:** Think of the last review or feedback session on a piece of work you were involved in. What question was it actually designed to answer — approval or task completion? Was the session designed to produce the type of evidence that answers that question?
