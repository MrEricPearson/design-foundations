# Decision Fatigue
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 256, 247 | **Cluster:** J — Cognitive Science

**Goal:** Recognize when decision fatigue — the depletion of deliberate reasoning capacity after many sequential decisions — is degrading the quality of decisions being made, including your own.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that decision quality is a matter of attention and care — a person who pays attention and takes the decision seriously will make consistent decisions regardless of when in a session the decision occurs. The correction: System 2 deliberation is a finite resource. It depletes with use across a session, and as depletion increases, S1 takes over more of the decision-making. Post-depletion decisions are not worse in the sense of less careful — they feel just as considered — but they are structurally different: more automatic, more influenced by defaults and whatever is most salient, and more likely to produce either impulsive choices ("just decide something") or conservative defaults ("approve nothing, change nothing").

The mechanism: this is distinct from Hick's Law (256), which describes how the number of options at a single decision point increases decision time and difficulty. Decision Fatigue describes the temporal depletion effect: what happens after many decisions in sequence, regardless of how many options each presented. A person who has made twenty small decisions in a meeting is not the same cognitive entity as that person making decision one. The twenty-first decision is made by a depleted S2, which means it's largely made by S1. This has a predictable signature: late-session decisions cluster toward either the most salient option or the status quo.

**You'll see it when:** Important decisions placed at the end of a long agenda receive less deliberation than they deserve and tend to default to the path of least resistance. Meeting participants who seemed engaged and thoughtful for the first hour become more agreeable (or more resistant) by hour three without their positions having actually changed. Design reviews that proceed through many small style decisions before reaching the consequential structural decision produce low-quality engagement on the one that mattered most.

**The signal:** A significant decision made late in a long session produces agreement that doesn't match the question's complexity. Or the opposite: the group resists any decision and defers indefinitely, not because the decision is unclear but because making a decision has become cognitively costly. Late-session "let's just go with X" or "let's discuss this next time" are both signatures of depleted S2, not of the underlying difficulty of the question.

**Don't confuse this with:** Legitimate fatigue from a difficult decision, or genuine agreement after good deliberation. The false positive: a room that reaches quick consensus late in a meeting may genuinely have agreed — or may have arrived at a fatigued default. The test is whether the same decision, made first in the session, would receive more scrutiny or produce a different outcome. If the answer is yes, decision fatigue is the variable.

**Try Noticing:** In the next long meeting, pay attention to the agenda order: which decisions came first, which came last, and what quality of engagement each received. Notice whether the post-lunch or end-of-agenda decisions received the same depth of discussion as the opening items. Then notice the outcomes: did the late decisions feel settled, or were they the ones that got reopened the following week?

**What Next:** 256 (Hick's Law) for the complementary mechanism — too many options per decision is distinct from too many decisions in sequence, but both degrade S2 engagement. 314 (How People Actually Decide) Part 3 for the arc-level method on structuring decision meetings to put consequential decisions first and reduce cumulative depletion.
