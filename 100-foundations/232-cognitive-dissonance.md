# Cognitive Dissonance
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 101, 231 | **Episode:** 3

**Goal:** Recognize when users are rationalizing a behavior or decision after the fact, rather than describing the genuine reasoning that produced it — so you can distinguish stated rationale from actual causation.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that when users explain why they did something, the explanation reflects the actual reasoning that drove the decision. The correction: people experience discomfort when their actions conflict with their self-image or prior beliefs (cognitive dissonance), and they resolve that discomfort by revising their stated beliefs to align with the action — not by changing the action.

The mechanism: when someone does something that contradicts a belief they hold, the brain generates a revised belief that makes the behavior consistent with who they think they are. This revision is experienced as genuine — the person does not know they're rationalizing. They believe the post-hoc explanation they produce.

For design, this means that post-hoc user explanations — "I chose this because..." — often describe a justification that was constructed after the choice, not a cause that produced it. In usability sessions, when a user makes a mistake and is asked why, they will frequently produce a confident explanation that has no connection to the actual source of the error. In interviews about past behavior, the explanation users provide will be more coherent than the actual decision process was. The explanation is not false in the sense of being deliberately misleading; it is false in the sense of being constructed, not recalled.

The practical consequence: if you use post-hoc explanations to understand what drove a decision, you are studying the user's self-model, not the decision-making process. Self-models are worth studying — but they are not the same thing.

**You'll see it when:** A user in a research session confidently explains a decision they just made in a way that doesn't match what you observed driving that decision. Or in stakeholder discussions, after a direction has been chosen, team members produce explanations for why it was the right choice that were not part of the original rationale.

**The signal:** An explanation for a decision is cleaner, more coherent, and more internally consistent than the decision process you observed was.

**Don't confuse this with:** Deliberate post-hoc rationalization that users know they're doing. Cognitive dissonance operates below the threshold of conscious awareness — the user genuinely believes the explanation they're giving. This is important because it means challenging the explanation directly ("but I saw you do X") will not produce acknowledgment; it will produce more defensive rationalization. The productive response is to separate observation ("I noticed you clicked here") from interpretation ("what were you looking for?") and let the user's language reveal the actual state.

**Try Noticing:** In your most recent research session: were there moments where a user's explanation of their behavior didn't match what you observed driving it? Write down what you observed and what they said separately. If the two are coherent, that's a signal worth following. If the explanation is neater than the behavior, that gap is your data.
