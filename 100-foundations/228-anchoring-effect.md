# Anchoring Effect
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 101, 227 | **Episode:** 3

**Goal:** Recognize when a first-seen value, option, or frame is disproportionately shaping subsequent judgment — in your team's decisions and in your users' decisions.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that people evaluate options based on the options themselves. The correction: people evaluate options relative to a reference point, and the first reference point they encounter anchors their judgment in ways they don't recognize and can't easily override.

The mechanism: when the brain encounters a value or option, it establishes it as a reference point and then adjusts from it. Adjustments are systematically insufficient — people move away from the anchor, but not far enough. The anchor's pull persists even when people know it's irrelevant. In pricing studies, people told to write down a random number before estimating a price will give estimates correlated with that number, even when they know the number was random.

This operates in two distinct ways that matter for design. First, in designer and team decisions: the first concept shown in a design review becomes the implicit standard all subsequent concepts are measured against. The first estimate given in a planning session anchors the range of what feels reasonable. The first user complaint heard anchors perception of the overall problem space.

Second, in user decisions: the first price seen in a comparison makes subsequent prices feel cheap or expensive relative to it. The default option anchors what feels like "normal." The first field in a form anchors the cognitive register users apply to the rest of it.

**You'll see it when:** In design reviews, feedback on later concepts systematically references the first one shown ("compared to the earlier version..."). In pricing or feature tiers, a high-priced option makes the next tier feel affordable regardless of its absolute value. In research, the finding from the first participant disproportionately influences how subsequent participants' findings are interpreted.

**The signal:** Comparisons are consistently framed relative to whatever was seen first, rather than evaluated against an independent standard.

**Don't confuse this with:** Loss aversion (229), which is about the asymmetric weight of losses versus gains. Anchoring is about the reference point that makes something feel like a gain or loss in the first place. The two interact: what counts as "losing" depends on the anchor that defined the baseline.

**Try Noticing:** Review the last design review or decision session in your current project. What was shown or proposed first? Look at the feedback on everything that came after — does it reference that first option as a baseline, even implicitly? Now look at a user decision in your product: what's the first option or value the user sees, and how does it frame everything that follows?

**What Next:** Read 229 (Loss Aversion) — anchoring sets the reference point that defines what counts as a gain or loss, so the two biases work together to shape user decisions. If you're designing decision flows, pricing structures, or comparison interfaces, both are active simultaneously.
