# What a Pain Point Is
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Foundational prereq for all journey mapping pieces (206c, 206a, 206b); pairs with 153 (Experience vs. Process)

**Goal:** Recognize a genuine pain point in a user experience — so you can distinguish it from a preference, a feature request, or a surface complaint.

**Concept:** A pain point is a moment in an experience where the friction costs the user something: time they didn't plan to spend, effort they didn't expect to exert, or a failure they didn't anticipate. It's not a negative opinion about a feature. It's a specific moment with a specific cost — and the cost is what makes it a pain point rather than feedback.

**You'll see it when:** Someone describes not just that something was frustrating but what they had to do as a result. "I couldn't find the export button, so I had to ask IT every time I needed the data" — that's a pain point. The friction is specific; the cost is specific. "I wish the dashboard were cleaner" — that's a preference. The frustration is real, but there's no observable cost yet.

**The signal:** The cost is specific and observable. Repeated workarounds, extended task times, task abandonment, or stopping to ask for help are behavioral signals of pain points. General dissatisfaction without a specific behavioral cost is feedback or a preference — valuable, but a different kind of signal.

**Don't confuse this with:** A feature request — "I wish this had X" — which describes what's absent. A pain point describes what's costly in what exists. Both are valuable inputs; they drive different kinds of decisions. A feature request expands scope; a pain point identifies where existing scope is failing.

**Try Noticing:** Think of the last time you watched someone use something and felt like they were working harder than they should have. What specifically were they doing that produced that extra effort? The specific moment and its cost is the pain point — not the general sense that "this could be easier."

**What Next:** When you're marking pain points on a journey map, use any of the 206 approach pieces. When you're evaluating whether a pain point is worth addressing, the size of the pain (frequency × severity × cost) is the relevant signal — not the intensity of the user's expressed frustration.
