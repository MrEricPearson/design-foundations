# Constraints as Design Input
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 109 | **Episode:** 5

**Goal:** Recognize when a constraint is functioning as a simplification tool rather than just a limitation — so you can use it as a forcing function instead of only managing it as an obstacle.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that constraints are obstacles to route around — the goal is to achieve as much as possible despite them. The correction: constraints force the elimination of options, and when they eliminate genuinely weak options, they function as a design tool. The mechanism: unlimited options don't produce better solutions; they produce more deliberation with the same outcome distribution. A time constraint, a technology limitation, or a scope boundary forces a question that unlimited capacity never forces: what is actually necessary here? That question surfaces priorities that discussion often can't. The constraint doesn't add anything — it removes what you were avoiding having to decide about.

The important nuance: not every constraint helps. Some eliminate good options along with weak ones, and the honest answer is that the constraint is just limiting. The question is not "is this secretly good?" but "what does this force me to decide?" The answer is informative either way.

**You'll see it when:** A team is circling a decision that has too many options and no clear priority — and a deadline, resource limit, or technical constraint is present that nobody has examined as a scope decision rather than an obstacle.

**The signal:** The team's conversation about the constraint is about how to get around it or absorb it, not about what it forces out of scope and whether that's actually a problem.

**Don't confuse this with optimism bias.** The reframe is not "every constraint is secretly good." A false positive: asking "what does this force me to simplify?" and getting an answer you like feels like the constraint validated itself. But the test is whether the simplified version solves the actual problem — not whether it feels manageable. If the constraint cuts a capability that was genuinely necessary, the constraint is a limitation, not a simplification, and that's worth naming explicitly.

**Try Noticing:** Name one active constraint in your current work. Ask: what does this force out? Is what it forces out genuinely necessary, or is it something you were including out of habit, expectation, or coverage anxiety?
