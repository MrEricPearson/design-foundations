# Not All Assumptions Are Equal
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize (Practice Atom) | **Arc:** The Risk in What You Assume, Part 2 | **Prereqs:** 100 | **Episode:** 1

**Goal:** Recognize which assumptions in your current work actually warrant stopping — so you can prioritize verification rather than either freezing everything or ignoring everything.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that surfacing an assumption means you need to verify it before moving forward. The correction: assumptions create different amounts of risk, and treating them identically produces one of two failure modes — halting work on assumptions that turn out not to matter, or treating a project-ending assumption as equivalent to a color preference. The mechanism: risk isn't a property of the assumption itself; it's a product of two independent variables. Impact measures how much it costs if you're wrong. Uncertainty measures how confident you actually are. A high-uncertainty assumption with low impact is safe to move with; a high-impact assumption with high uncertainty is the one that derails projects when left unexamined.

**The move:** For each flagged assumption, rate it on two dimensions — impact (high/low: if this is wrong, how much does it cost?) and uncertainty (high/low: how confident are you, really?). High impact + high uncertainty = address before building on it. Everything else can proceed with appropriate acknowledgment.

**Don't confuse impact with urgency.** A high-urgency assumption isn't necessarily the high-risk one — urgency is time pressure, impact is consequence of being wrong. Ranking by deadline proximity instead of impact means the squeaky wheel gets addressed and the structural risk gets buried. A false positive: a short list that has been sorted feels like progress. But if the sort was by urgency rather than impact × uncertainty, the highest-ranked item may be the most time-sensitive one, not the most consequential one — and those are often different assumptions.

**Try Noticing:** Look at the assumptions currently driving your work. Which ones are you treating with equal weight? For the assumption you're least likely to question, ask: what does it actually cost if this turns out to be wrong? That cost is your impact score.

**What Next:** If a high-impact assumption feels hard to question even after naming it, read 102 (Bias as Assumption) — that's the next layer, where the assumption has become invisible enough that you can't see it from inside the work.
