# Reading Patterns
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 188 | **Note:** Prereq for 191 (typography as hierarchy); critical for layout decisions on information-dense screens

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to recognize when content is positioned outside the natural scan path — and understand why "it's on the page" is not the same as "users will find it."

**Concept:** Users do not read screens. They scan screens. The distinction is not pedantic — it changes what content placement means entirely. Reading is linear and complete. Scanning is selective and pattern-driven. Users apply a scan pattern within the first few hundred milliseconds of seeing a screen, before they've read a word, to assess where the relevant content likely is. Only after the scan do they slow down to read.

Two patterns dominate in digital interfaces:

**The F-pattern** appears on text-heavy pages. The eye moves horizontally across the top of the content area, then drops and moves horizontally across a shorter span, then drops again and moves vertically down the left side. The result: top-left content gets the most attention; right-side content after the first line gets significantly less; content below the fold gets very little unless something on the page earns the scroll. This is not a preference — it is a behavior documented across thousands of eye-tracking studies on text-dominant pages.

**The Z-pattern** appears on pages with a clear action orientation — fewer blocks of text, more distinct elements. The eye starts at the top-left, moves across to the top-right, then diagonals down-left, then sweeps across to the bottom-right. Navigation lives at top-left and top-right. The primary action lives at bottom-right, at the end of the diagonal path. This is why call-to-action buttons are conventionally placed at the bottom right of forms: the eye arrives there naturally after completing the Z.

The mechanism underlying both: the brain has efficient scanning heuristics developed through years of consuming content in a particular medium. Whoever controls content placement can work with those heuristics (placing the highest-priority content in the highest-attention positions) or against them (placing it anywhere that "fits," regardless of whether the eye goes there).

**You'll see it when:** Analytics show low engagement with content that is visually prominent but positioned in low-attention zones. A call-to-action "not getting clicks" that is well-designed but placed in the lower-left quadrant of an F-pattern page. Users "not seeing" content that exists but isn't in the scan path. Or: content that tests well when moved, without changing the content itself — only the placement.

**The signal:** A designer or stakeholder says "but the information is right there" — pointing to content that users consistently miss. "Right there" means "visible in the design." It does not mean "in the scan path." These are different.

**Don't confuse scan patterns with reading behavior** — scan patterns describe the initial assessment of a page, not deep engagement. A user who decides a page is worth reading will move past their initial scan pattern and read more thoroughly. The scan pattern determines whether they make that decision. Content outside the scan path must be important enough to earn the scroll or the deliberate search — most content is not.

**Try Noticing:** Look at a text-heavy page — a long dashboard, a news feed, an internal tool with a lot of content. Trace the likely F-pattern on that page: where would the eye go? Now look at what content is positioned at those high-attention points. Is it the highest-priority content? Or did layout convenience put it there? Now look at the content positioned in low-attention zones — right side of the screen below the first horizontal sweep, lower left below the fold. What is there? Does it matter? If it matters, it's being served poorly.
