# Question Design and Question Bias
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 102 | **Episode:** 11

**Goal:** Recognize when a research question confirms what you already believe rather than revealing what users actually experience — so you can apply a structural fix before the question contaminates the data.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that a question sounds neutral, so it produces neutral data. The correction: every question contains an assumption embedded in its phrasing, and that assumption shapes what answers are possible. The mechanism: a loaded question ("how frustrating is it when X happens?") assumes the user has experienced X, that it was frustrating, and that the relevant variable is degree of frustration — three assumptions before the user has said a word. A participant who's never experienced X or found it irrelevant is not accommodated by the question's structure. The answer that comes back — even a carefully considered one — is filtered through those assumptions rather than drawn from the participant's actual experience. The problem is invisible to whoever wrote the question because the assumption was already inside their thinking when they drafted it.

Two structural tests:
1. **Assumption test:** Does the question assume the user has done, felt, or encountered something specific? Questions with "when X happens..." / "how much do you..." / "wouldn't it be better if..." assume the experience before it's confirmed. Neutral phrasings: "walk me through a time when..." / "what happens when..." / "how do you typically..."
2. **Open/closed test:** Could the user respond "I've never experienced that" or "I don't think about it that way"? Questions that close off those responses force the user to answer within your frame.

**You'll see it when:** Interview findings produce strong validation of a hypothesis with no contradictory evidence. Or when survey data shows highly consistent agreement across all respondents — which is statistically unusual in genuine open inquiry.

**The signal:** The findings from the research look exactly like what the team expected to find. This is a red flag, not a confirmation — genuine inquiry almost always surfaces at least some unexpected findings.

**Don't confuse question bias with intentional leading.** Most biased questions are written by careful people trying to be precise. The bias comes from writing questions from inside a hypothesis you've already formed. A false positive: rewriting a biased question to sound more neutral feels like it addressed the problem. But if the rewrite still assumes the scenario that motivated the original question, the structure changed while the embedded assumption didn't.

**Try Noticing:** Take three interview questions you've used or plan to use. Apply both tests to each. For any question that fails the open/closed test, ask: what would a participant need to be true about their experience to give an answer that surprises you?

**What Next:** Once your questions are clean, read 202a (Conversational Interview) for the synchronous interview method. If you're designing survey questions, the same bias tests apply at scale — a leading survey question contaminates every response that follows it.
