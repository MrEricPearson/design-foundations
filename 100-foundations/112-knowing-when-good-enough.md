# Knowing When Good Enough Is Actually Good Enough
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** 103, 113 | **Episode:** 2

**Goal:** Recognize the difference between a real improvement and a preference-driven change — so you can stop refining on purpose rather than by running out of time.

**Concept:** The working assumption is that more refinement always produces better work — that if you have time to improve something, you should. The correction: refinement follows diminishing returns, and at some point additional changes satisfy preferences rather than solve problems. The mechanism: when a direction is fundamentally right, the changes that remain are improvements of degree, not corrections of kind. They make the work more polished, but they don't change what the work gets right or wrong. The trap is that preference-driven changes feel necessary from the inside — the eye catches something, the instinct is to fix it, and it's genuinely hard to distinguish "I notice this isn't right" from "I notice this isn't exactly how I'd do it." The test that separates them: can you describe what problem the next change solves? If the answer is about aesthetics or taste rather than a gap between what the work does and what it needs to do, it's a preference.

This is categorically different from attachment (103). Attachment is clinging to a direction that may be fundamentally wrong. Good-enough is knowing when a fundamentally right direction doesn't need more time.

**You'll see it when:** Refinement continues past a point where reviewers can articulate what was improved. Changes cycle back to earlier states. The work is "almost done" for an unusually long time.

**The signal:** The next planned change is motivated by personal taste rather than a named gap between the work's current state and what it needs to do.

**Don't confuse this with lowering standards.** "Good enough" means the next round of changes addresses preferences rather than problems. A false positive: stopping feels irresponsible, and the "one more pass" feels like diligence. But diligence that produces preference-driven changes is a cost to the work — not in quality, but in time and attention that could address something that's actually a problem. The test is the specific change in front of you, not the general commitment to quality.

**Try Noticing:** Name one thing you're currently refining. For the next planned change, ask before making it: "What specific problem does this solve?" If the answer is a preference rather than a gap, notice whether you were planning to make the change anyway.

**What Next:** If what's keeping you refining is actually attachment rather than genuine improvement potential, read 103 (Attachment Is the Real Risk). If you want a quick structural check before calling something done, read 111 (Self-Critique).
