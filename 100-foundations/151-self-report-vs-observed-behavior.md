# Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior
**Tier:** 100 — Recognize | **Note:** Foundational prereq for 202 (Research Mechanics) and 301 (From Vague Ask to Real Persona); pairs with 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude)

**Goal:** Recognize when research data is what people said they do versus what they were observed doing — and why that difference matters for decisions.

**Concept:** Self-report is what participants tell you: their memories, descriptions, preferences, and predictions about their own behavior. Observed behavior is what they actually do when you watch. The gap between them is consistent: people describe idealized or remembered versions of their behavior, not accurate ones. Both types of data are useful — they answer different questions.

**You'll see it when:** Research findings are based entirely on interviews or surveys, and those findings are being used to predict what users will do in a product. The data may accurately represent what participants believe about themselves. It may not accurately predict how they'll actually behave in context.

**The signal:** The research method never puts participants in a situation where you can watch them do the thing. Surveys and interviews produce self-report data by construction — not because they're bad methods, but because that's what they're designed to gather.

**Don't confuse this with:** Dishonesty — participants in self-report studies aren't misrepresenting themselves on purpose. The gap between self-report and observed behavior is usually about memory accuracy, self-presentation, and the difference between what people intend to do in an imagined scenario and what they actually do in a real one. It's a structural limitation of the method, not a credibility problem with the participants.

**Try Noticing:** Look at the evidence behind a decision you're involved in. What percentage of it is what people said they do versus what was observed? If the decision depends on predicting behavior, how much of the evidence was generated by watching behavior happen?

**What Next:** When you're deciding which research approach fits your question, read 202 (Research Mechanics) — the three approaches differ specifically in how they generate self-report versus observed data and in what access they require.
