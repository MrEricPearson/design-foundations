Act I—The Frame3 episodes

Establishes the organizing argument before any method content: where risk lives, what to do before starting, and who you're building for. Every subsequent episode is a tool in the derisking toolkit introduced here.

01
The Risk in What You Assume
100 What an assumption is101 Assumption vs. fact102 When assumptions harden103 What an experiment is104 Your derisking toolkit105 What iteration is
Understand
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners hold a thoroughness model: more planning before starting = less risk. They've seen rushing go wrong and treat "slow down and think it through" as the fix.
Schema disrupted
The problem isn't where you start — it's emotional attachment before you've tested anything. You can start anywhere. Risk accumulates when you can no longer afford to be wrong.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — new frame requires restructuring. Extraneous: low — no procedure. Germane: high — four-tool overview scaffolds the entire series.
After this episode
Can name which decisions in any current work item are tested vs. untested; can identify when emotional attachment to a direction has arrived before any testing has; can use the four derisking tools as a selection menu when deciding what piece to read next. Without this frame, every subsequent episode reads as an isolated technique — with it, each one is immediately recognizable as a tool for a specific risk.
Sequencing rationale
Must be first. Without it, all other episodes read as isolated techniques. With it, every subsequent episode is immediately recognizable as a tool for a specific kind of derisking.
02
Before You Begin
137 The quality ladder138 Delight as behavioral trust106 Sketching / quick visualization113 Defining success before you start114 Time-boxing as constraint-setting122 Starting questions207 Premortem
Apply
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners start work by opening tools — Figma, a doc, Jira. Pre-work feels like ceremony that slows things down. The implicit model: starting is how you find out what you need to know.
Schema disrupted
Five minutes before anything is opened, there's a set of checks that determine the quality of every decision that follows. Skipping them doesn't accelerate work — it defers a cost that shows up later at higher effort.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: low — 5 named checks in a familiar format. Germane: high — the premortem pairs directly with Ep3 to close the assumption-awareness loop before work opens.
After this episode

Sequencing rationale
Second — Ep1 established where risk lives. This episode establishes what you're derisking toward: a specific quality level, not just "done." The quality ladder (137) and trust frame (138) answer the question Ep1 opens but doesn't close: what does good actually look like?
03
The Person You're Building For
107 Framing the problem123 What usability testing is125 Jobs-to-be-done126 Mental models173 Empathy as cognitive simulation301c Assumption-first proto-persona301 Arc: Vague ask → real persona
ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners already think in terms of "users" — a demographic model: enterprise PMs, mid-market, 35–50. Functional but so low-resolution that design decisions built on it rest almost entirely on assumption.
Schema disrupted
Users aren't demographics — they're people in situations with specific jobs. "Our users" hides the variation design decisions need to navigate. A persona is a testable proposition, not a statistical average.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — JTBD requires shifting from "who" to "what they're trying to accomplish." Germane: high — the 301 arc produces a proto-persona with every assumption made explicit.
After this episode
Can move from "our users want X" to "a person in [situation] is trying to [job], and we haven't tested [specific assumption] about them." The persona artifact carries explicit confidence levels.
Sequencing rationale
Third — closing Act I. The derisking frame and pre-work checks are established. Apply them immediately to the most fundamental question: who is this for? Act II follows to interrogate what kind of evidence that answer rests on.
Act II—Behavior and Ethics4 episodes

Builds the behavioral science and ethical foundations every design decision rests on. Every interface is a claim about how people behave; these four episodes provide the vocabulary for making that claim responsibly.

04
What Kind of Evidence Is This?
148 Primary vs. secondary research149 Research vs. anecdote150 Pattern vs. data point151 Self-report vs. observed behavior152 Behavior vs. attitude155 Synthesis vs. analysis156 Name what people do, not what they think
UnderstandAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners treat "research" as a single category — user interviews. The question is scheduling, not methodology. A PM's call notes and 12 structured behavioral interviews carry equal decision weight.
Schema disrupted
Evidence comes in types, and each type answers a different question. Using behavioral evidence to answer an attitudinal question produces the right-shaped answer to the wrong question.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — 7 distinction pairs. Germane: high — the classification framework applies retroactively to every piece of evidence the practitioner has ever cited.
After this episode
Can classify any evidence type, state what question it answers, and name what it cannot tell you. Can point to the gap in an evidence base before committing to a direction.
Sequencing rationale
Fourth — before the bias episodes. Understanding what kind of evidence exists is prerequisite to understanding how biases contaminate each type differently. The contamination map only works with a typed evidence model.

Prior knowledge activated

07
The Ethics of Knowing
233 Dark patterns234 Persuasion vs. manipulation235 Gamification principles236 Intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation222 Reward loop design307 Arc: Dark Pattern Awareness
AnalyzeEvaluate
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners know "dark patterns" as a term. Ethical design is treated as a separate compliance layer — something legal or accessibility teams worry about, distinct from everyday design decisions.
Schema disrupted
The same cognitive mechanisms that make you a better designer also enable exploitation. Ethics isn't separate from the psychology — it's the flip side of the same coin. The mechanism is identical; the intent and design goal differ.
Cognitive load

Sequencing rationale
Seventh — closing Act II. The cognitive science in Ep6 is the direct referent for this ethical frame. The persuasion/manipulation line only makes sense after the mechanisms are understood. Acts I–II now form a complete behavioral foundation.
Act III—Structure4 episodes

Establishes what the practitioner is working within and building toward before any visual design begins: decisions already made, structural content the system knows about, how users navigate to it, and how to write about it.

08
What the System Already Decided
110 Constraints as design input166 Default as a design decision193 Decision inventory194 Three constraint types195 Design system as encoded model196 Where design systems fail
Analyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners experience constraints as friction — the design system won't let them do what they need. Constraints read as limits on the ideal design rather than as design material.
Schema disrupted
The system has already made decisions — from vendors, platforms, prior teams — before the practitioner touches a file. Constraints are design material. Defaults are design decisions too. The question is which type each constraint is and what response that requires.
Cognitive load

Sequencing rationale
Eighth — opening Act III after behavioral science. The human dimension and ethical frame are established. Now establish the system dimension: what has already been decided, what the defaults are.

What the System Knows
179 What a content model is180 Content types and attributes181 Structured vs. unstructured content182 The bolt-on cost183 Classification decisions compound184 Labeling systems in IA185 Metadata as design186 Taxonomy design basics197 Naming as model design221 Content modeling method
UnderstandApply
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners think in screens and features. They have implicit content models but have never named them. They've encountered the failure mode — a platform that can't do what the design assumes — without knowing its structural cause.
Schema disrupted
You don't design screens — you design a content model that generates screens. Every attribute is a capability decision. What you don't define as a type, you can never filter, sort, relate, or surface.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: high — genuinely new discipline with no prior referent. Load is relieved by ending with method 221: vocabulary is applied before it decays.
After this episode
Can identify a product's content types, attributes, and the gaps where the model doesn't support the claimed capabilities. Can run the 5-step content modeling method before screen design begins. The content model is the artifact through which users' mental models either do or don't match the product — when the taxonomy doesn't match how users categorize their domain, no amount of navigation design compensates because the mismatch is structural.
Sequencing rationale
Ninth — after constraint mapping. Here's what's been decided (Ep8) → here's what the content model underneath looks like, and why changing it later costs what it costs (Ep9). Information architecture (Ep10) follows: you must know what content exists before designing how users navigate to it.

Information Architecture
172 Browse vs. search204a Task-based IA204b Content-based IA218 Card sorting220 Working in vendor software
UnderstandApply
Prior knowledge activated
Navigation is designed last — after screens exist and someone asks "how do users find this?" IA is a sitemap: a tree organized by what the product contains rather than what users arrive to do.
Schema disrupted
Browse vs. search isn't a UI pattern choice — it's a claim about how users form intent. Task-based and content-based IA express different mental models. Getting the wrong one means reorganizing the entire navigation after users fail in production.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — two IA strategy types plus four methods. Germane: high — card sorting directly tests the assumed IA against real user categorizations.
After this episode
Can choose an IA strategy based on user intent rather than content inventory; can run a card sort to validate the navigation structure; can evaluate vendor platform IA against the same criteria. IA that doesn't match users' mental models produces navigation that works for the designer and fails in production — card sorting's value is in where participants' groupings diverge from the planned structure, not where they confirm it.
Sequencing rationale
Tenth — immediately after content modeling. You must know what content exists before designing how users navigate to it. Content model precedes IA; IA precedes interface copy (Ep11). This three-episode sequence closes before any visual design begins.

Act IV—Perception4 episodes

Builds the perceptual and cognitive vocabulary practitioners need to evaluate, prototype, or improve anything visual. Moves from pre-attentive processing through attention capacity and interface states, closing with data literacy.

Visual Hierarchy and Perception
133 Visual hierarchy187 Layout principles (PARC)188 Gestalt principles189 Whitespace as signal190 Reading patterns191 Typography as hierarchy192 Color as communication
Understand
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners have aesthetic intuitions — "this doesn't feel right" or "this looks cluttered" — but no vocabulary for diagnosing why or prescribing a fix. Visual feedback from stakeholders arrives in the same vocabulary: preferences, not principles.
Schema disrupted
The eye processes information before cognition begins. Pre-attentive features — proximity, contrast, alignment, grouping — determine what gets seen and in what order. Visual hierarchy is not aesthetic preference; it is a claim about the sequence of attention.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — multiple principles unified by one claim (pre-attentive processing). Germane: high — the principles convert immediately into diagnostic questions practitioners can apply to any interface.
After this episode
Can audit any interface for hierarchy failures using five named principles; can explain visual decisions using a shared vocabulary rather than aesthetic preference; can give and receive design feedback that is diagnostic rather than evaluative.
Sequencing rationale
Twelfth — opening Act IV. Structure (Acts I–III) is complete. Now build perceptual vocabulary. Visual hierarchy first because it underlies every subsequent perception concept: progressive disclosure (Ep13) and states (Ep14) both rely on hierarchy as their substrate.

States, Forms, and Onboarding
116 Error states as a trust system117 Empty states118 Edge and boundary states240 Form design principles241 Empty states (canonical)242 Error states and messages (canonical)243 Onboarding design patterns213 Accessibility checklist for forms223 Form design method
ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Error states are edge cases — something went wrong, show a message. Empty states are filler. Forms are fields arranged by database schema. Each is treated as a separate UI concern handled late in the design process.
Schema disrupted
States are the full experience of the system — not edge cases. Every state is either a trust-building or trust-destroying moment. Forms sequence user willingness, not database schema. Onboarding reveals whether the first-run experience closes the intent-disclosure gap.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: high — 9 pieces across atomic concepts and methods. Managed by the state taxonomy (error/empty/edge) as the organizing frame. Germane: high — form design method (223) produces a real form during the episode.
After this episode

Sequencing rationale
Fourteenth — states are the application of progressive disclosure to real interface conditions. The three perception episodes (12, 13, 14) form a closed system: how vision works, how to manage capacity, and how to design every state the user encounters.

Reading Data Clearly
170 Exploratory vs. explanatory visualization261 Correlation vs. causation262 Statistical vs. practical significance263 Selection bias264 Survivorship bias212 Data visualization for decision-makers316 Arc: Reading Data Without Getting Fooled
AnalyzeEvaluate
Prior knowledge activated
Data means charts. A chart in a dashboard review is treated as evidence. "The data shows X" is treated as equivalent to "X is true." Practitioners accept the chart's authority without examining its construction.
Schema disrupted
The chart is not evidence — it's a representation of a sample with selection criteria, collection timing, and visualization choices that determine what it can and cannot show. Most dashboard reads are more authoritative than the underlying data warrants.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — 4 data bias types plus 2 visualization modes. Germane: high — Arc 316 requires applying the skeptic's frame to a real analytics dashboard during the session.
After this episode
Can identify correlation/causation conflation, selection bias, survivorship bias, and the statistical/practical significance gap in any data claim; can choose between exploratory and explanatory visualization based on the audience's decision state.
Sequencing rationale
Fifteenth — closing Act IV. Visual hierarchy (Ep12), progressive systems (Ep13), and interface states (Ep14) addressed how we see and interpret visual interfaces. This episode addresses the data dimension of perception, completing the perceptual vocabulary before the evidence act begins.
Act V—Evidence3 episodes

Bridges understanding (Acts I–IV) and production (Act VI). Behavioral concepts and data literacy are established; these three episodes apply them by running real research methods, mapping user journeys, and evaluating the quality of what was gathered.

Research in Practice
159 Observation effect202a Conversational interview202b Remote / async research202c Observational research
Apply
Prior knowledge activated
Interviews are about asking good questions. The researcher sits across from someone and asks about their experience. Remote interviews are the same thing on video. Observation is watching someone use a product.
Schema disrupted
The researcher's presence and behavior contaminate results in predictable, preventable ways. Observation effect is not a philosophical concern — it is a systematic bias you can design out by changing what you do before and during the session.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — observation effect as a frame plus 3 method types. Extraneous: low — method steps live in the T200 pieces. Germane: high — each method type is practiced during the episode.
After this episode
Can run a conversational interview, remote/async research session, or observational research session without the most common contamination patterns; can select the right method for the question being asked. Each research method is a mechanism for converting assumptions into evidence — the contamination patterns this episode addresses are precisely how anecdote replaces evidence: a leading question produces the interviewer's beliefs, not the participant's experience.
Sequencing rationale
Sixteenth — first episode of Act V. The behavioral concepts (Act II) and data literacy (Act IV close) are established; now run real methods. Episode 17 follows immediately to apply the observational output to journey mapping.

Journey Maps — Three Ways
153 Experience vs. process154 What a pain point is165 Frontstage and backstage175 What a journey map is176 Swimlanes as a comparison tool206a Discovery-driven journey map206b Co-creative journey map206c Assumption-first proto-map
ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners think of journey maps as a single thing: a linear timeline of user steps with pain points. The question they bring is "when do we make the journey map?" rather than "which version serves this question?"
Schema disrupted
A journey map is not a format — it's a research or collaboration artifact, and there are three fundamentally different kinds depending on what you already know. The frontstage/backstage distinction reveals what the map cannot show without it: the systems that make the experience possible.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: high — 3 distinct methods with different inputs, processes, and outputs. Managed by a decision-tree format: what do you already know? That question routes to the right method.
After this episode

Sequencing rationale
Seventeenth — after research in practice (Ep16). Journey maps are built from research output. Placing this episode immediately after the methods episode means practitioners apply what they just gathered. Episode 18 follows to evaluate the rigor of what was gathered.

Research Rigor and Measurement
131 Question design / question bias160 What a baseline is161 Metric vs. measure162 The interpretation gap244 Qualitative vs. quantitative research245 Mixed methods246 Sample size and what it means
AnalyzeEvaluate
Prior knowledge activated
Qualitative means opinions; quantitative means numbers. Sample size is how many people you talked to. Research is evaluated by whether the team felt confident afterward.
Schema disrupted
Question design is a research contamination risk — how you ask changes what you hear. The interpretation gap is the space between what data says and what it means. A baseline is the level from which change is measured, not the current state.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: high — 7 interconnected concepts. Managed by sequencing: methodology classification (qual/quant/mixed) first, then the four quality evaluators (question bias, baseline, metric, interpretation gap).
After this episode
Can design research questions that don't contaminate responses; can distinguish qualitative, quantitative, and mixed evidence types; can set a true baseline and identify the interpretation gap; can evaluate sample size claims at the right level of precision.
Sequencing rationale
Eighteenth — closing Act V. The methods (Ep16) and journey maps (Ep17) have been run. This episode establishes the evaluative frame for assessing the quality of what was gathered. Act VI (Production) begins with the practitioner holding both research output and tools to evaluate it.
Act VI—Production5 episodes

The practitioner now makes things. Explore options before committing; use AI as a production accelerator; choose and sequence prototypes; test what was built; synthesize with others. The full derisking toolkit is in active use.

Exploring Options Before Committing
224 Quality audit108 Five Whys211 Quick competitive scan334 Arc: Solution-first rapid ideation
ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
You start with a solution in mind. Five Whys is for post-mortems. Competitive analysis is for the product roadmap. Ideation means brainstorming, which teams skip because everyone already knows what they're building.
Schema disrupted
Ideation tools aren't for creativity — they're for preventing attachment to the first direction. Starting with a solution is fine; the risk is never checking whether other solutions exist. Solution-first rapid ideation uses the current solution as the starting point, not something to avoid.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: low — 3 short, observable methods. Germane: high — all three methods are run against the same current work item during the episode, producing immediate comparative output.
After this episode
Can use 3 option-expansion methods before committing to any direction; can check a solution against its underlying problem using Five Whys; can generate comparative options rapidly without losing judgment authority over the result. Option expansion breaks anchoring (252) — the first solution considered becomes the implicit standard all alternatives are measured against; generating alternatives before evaluating any of them prevents the first-option anchor from filtering what gets considered.
Sequencing rationale
Nineteenth — opening Act VI. The foundations, behavioral science, structure, perception, and evidence are established. Now make things — but explore options before committing. Episode 20 follows: once a direction is chosen, use AI to accelerate production.

121 Environment setup for discovery in Cursor200 Story mapping in Cursor201 Grooming with AI219 AI for design work
Apply
Prior knowledge activated
AI helps produce things faster. Better prompt = better output. AI is the tool; the practitioner is the requester. Using AI means generating artifacts more quickly than doing it manually.
Schema disrupted
AI accelerates production; the bottleneck shifts to judgment clarity. You must know what you're asking for before AI can help produce it. Setup and framing quality determine output quality far more than prompt technique. AI does not close the judgment gap — it exposes it.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — environment setup plus 3 AI-assisted methods. Extraneous: moderate — Cursor setup requires following steps. Germane: high — story mapping in Cursor produces a real artifact during the episode.
After this episode

AI as execution partner (147, deepened in Ep27) decouples execution complexity from judgment complexity — the methods here are the first application of that principle: AI produces artifacts at scale, the practitioner's role shifts entirely to what to produce, not how.
Sequencing rationale
Twentieth — after option exploration. AI tools operate on a direction already chosen; using them before exploring options compounds attachment. Prototyping (Ep21) follows because the AI-produced artifacts are inputs to the prototype decision.

Prototyping — Choose and Sequence
132 Prototype fidelity177 What a prototype is178 Prototype vs. MVP309 Arc: Prototyping selection and sequencing309a Paper / sketch309b Lo-fi wireframe309c AI-generated309d Wizard of Oz309e Conversational309f High-fidelity309g Service prototype309h Parallel prototyping309i Build to think
ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners treat prototyping as a single activity — a clickable mockup in Figma. Higher fidelity reads as more professional and thorough. The question is when to make it, not which kind to make.
Schema disrupted
A prototype is a question, not a deliverable. Fidelity should match the question being tested, not the practitioner's confidence or the stakeholder's preference. High fidelity early destroys the signal it was supposed to generate.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: high — 9 distinct methods with different questions, audiences, and fidelity levels. Managed by the 309 arc's selection flowchart: what question are you testing? That routes to the right method.

Sequencing lower-fidelity types earlier isn't just about efficiency; it preserves the willingness to abandon a direction when evidence warrants it.
Sequencing rationale

Testing What You Built
124 Nielsen's 10 heuristics157 Why you don't help during testing158 Task statement design174 Think-aloud protocol200a A/B testing basics200b Iteration rhythm208 Five-second / first-click test215a Moderated usability session215b Unmoderated usability testing216 Heuristic evaluation
ApplyEvaluate
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners know roughly what usability testing is and often conflate "people reacted to it" with "we learned something." They may treat iteration as a continuous improvement loop without explicit exit conditions.
Schema disrupted
Feedback is not evidence. A reaction tells you what someone felt; a task completion tells you what someone can do. Heuristics (Nielsen's 10) give expert evaluation a shared vocabulary — violation identification, not preference reporting.
Cognitive load
Extraneous: high — 5 distinct test methods. Managed by the evidence-type frame from Ep4: each method maps to a question type practitioners already know how to classify.
After this episode

Heuristic evaluation can be run solo without participants. Testing reveals which quality threshold has been crossed: task completion signals usable, time-on-task signals efficient, unsolicited advocacy and workflow integration signal trusted. Every test is a designed encounter with a specific assumption — the practitioner who can name which assumption a result addresses before the test runs interprets it more accurately than one who enters testing hoping for validation.
Sequencing rationale
Twenty-second — immediately following prototyping to close the prototype/test loop. These two episodes (21–22) form the series' core production unit. The research rigor frame (Ep18) means practitioners arrive already knowing how to evaluate the quality of what they gather.

ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners have attended many meetings and know what brainstorming is. They think of facilitation as managing group dynamics — keeping energy up, managing the loudest voice. Self-critique feels like admitting something isn't done.

Sequencing rationale
Twenty-third — closing Act VI. Solo production capability (Ep19–22) is established; now bring others in. The workshop arc (304) produces inputs to work the practitioner will prototype and test again — creating the iterative loop the earlier production episodes describe.

Evidence into Decisions
109 Weighing trade-offs330 Arc: From Findings to Decisions331 Arc: The Good-Enough Evidence Problem332 Arc: Scope as a Design Decision
AnalyzeEvaluate
Prior knowledge activated
More evidence is always better. Research should precede every decision. "We need more research before we can move forward" is treated as a responsible, defensible position.
Schema disrupted
Evidence costs time; the real question is never "do we have enough?" — it's "would more evidence change this decision?" When the answer is no, more research delays rather than improves. Scope and trade-off decisions are made on incomplete evidence by design.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: low — 4 decision-making concepts. Germane: high — arcs 330, 331, 332 require making a real evidence-sufficiency call on a current decision during the session.
After this episode
Can make explicit trade-off decisions with named confidence levels; can identify when evidence is sufficient vs. when more would change the outcome; can make scope decisions based on user impact rather than convenience; can convert research findings into actionable decisions.
Sequencing rationale
Twenty-sixth — after service design and strategic transformation. The systems picture is complete. The remaining work is judgment under constraints: decisions made with incomplete but sufficient evidence. Episodes 27 and 28 close the series.

Designing with AI
147 AI as execution partner167 Automation bias and calibrated trust168 Explainability vs. transparency in AI169 Affordance302 Arc: Spotting AI Surprises and Agent Handoff308 Arc: Designing for AI Trust
ApplyAnalyze
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners have used AI tools and may have designed features that include AI. They approach "AI design" as optimizing outputs — more accurate prompts, faster generation cycles.
Schema disrupted
AI design is about designing the human experience of a system with uncertain, probabilistic outputs. A system right 95% of the time but communicating no uncertainty signal has a trust design failure, not an accuracy failure.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: moderate — three frames: automation bias, explainability requirements, handoff seams. Germane: moderate — both arcs require applying all three to a real AI-assisted workflow.
After this episode
Can evaluate an AI-assisted product's experience at three levels: trust (does the user know when and why to rely on this output?), explainability (can the user understand why the system produced this?), and handoff (where does the human take back control?).
Sequencing rationale
Twenty-seventh — after strategic judgment because designing for AI trust requires the same diagnostic posture. Also late because AI design amplifies existing design judgment rather than replacing it — the full prior toolkit is the referent.

Measurement, Debt, and Continuity
134 Design debt (the concept)135 Leading vs. lagging indicators209 Design decision records210 Retrospective for design decisions217 UX metrics300 Arc: The Cost You Don't See — Design Debt305 Did-it-work follow-up
AnalyzeEvaluate
Prior knowledge activated
Practitioners have shipped things and watched them degrade. They have a retrospective habit but apply it at project close — treating quality maintenance as an event with a defined end rather than a continuous discipline.
Schema disrupted
Design debt accrues continuously, not at project close. Every deviation from the intended model — however justified — is a debt entry. The question is not whether you took on debt but whether you know what, why, and when you plan to address it.
Cognitive load
Intrinsic: lower — concepts are familiar in principle. Germane: moderate — the habit loop requires setting up a lightweight decision record practice during or immediately after the episode while context is fresh.
After this episode
Has a habit loop: capture design decisions with rationale at the time they're made, surface design debt before it becomes unmanageable, run lightweight did-it-work follow-up after shipping, apply a continuous retrospective posture. The decision record practice survives team turnover in a way tribal knowledge doesn't. Design debt is the mechanism by which trusted products drift back down the quality ladder — a product doesn't have to get worse to lose trust; it just has to stop getting better while the world around it does.
Sequencing rationale
Last — deliberately so. The entire series teaches derisking through assumption-awareness. This episode teaches how to sustain that posture after the work ships, when there is no curriculum, no deadline, and no external prompt to examine assumptions. It is the capstone not because it is the hardest material but because it is the meta-skill.
