# Managing the Design Feedback Loop
**Tier:** 300 — Orchestrate | **Arc:** Full arc (4 parts) | **Audience:** PM-primary | **Prereqs:** 311 (Psychology of Stakeholder Decisions), 247 (Dual Process Theory), 249 (Loss Aversion), 129 (Stakeholder Mapping), 163 (Alignment vs. Consensus)

**Goal:** Run design reviews that produce decisions — by defining the right goal for each type of review session, classifying feedback in real time, closing on named decisions, and protecting approved work from erosion without dismissing legitimate new input.

**Trigger:** A design review that should have taken one session is now in its fourth round. Or: a decision that was approved two weeks ago is being reopened by someone who wasn't in the room. Or: every review ends with "let's think about it" and nothing is actually decided. The design work may be strong — but the review process is failing to move it forward.

---

## Part 1 — Defining the Goal Before the Meeting

**Concept:** Design reviews fail most often because no one said what kind of session it was. Three fundamentally different session types use the same name ("design review") and produce different outcomes when confused. An alignment session checks whether the direction is still correct — it's appropriate when significant work has been done since the last review. An approval session confirms that a defined outcome is ready to proceed — it requires specific, named criteria and a named decision-maker. A feedback session generates input on a direction that's still forming — it's appropriate early in a design cycle and should not produce a final decision.

When these are confused — when a feedback session ends with stakeholders expecting an approval outcome, or when an approval session reopens directional questions — iteration doesn't advance and trust erodes. The PM's job before a review is to know what type of session it is and say so.

**Method:**
Before any design review:
1. Name the session type: alignment, approval, or feedback — write it in the meeting title or the first line of the agenda
2. For alignment sessions: write the specific question being aligned on ("is this direction still right given what we know now?") and the participants who need to be present to make the alignment meaningful
3. For approval sessions: write the specific criteria for approval — what does the design need to demonstrate to proceed? Name who has approval authority. Communicate both before the session so criteria aren't introduced for the first time during the session.
4. For feedback sessions: write what's still open (what input would be useful) and what's already decided (what input would reopen settled work). Communicate the distinction at the start.

**What you end up with:** A review session where every participant knows what kind of session it is, what's being decided (or not), and what outcome closes the session.

**Proof:** If the session type isn't named and the criteria aren't pre-communicated, it becomes an approval session with the criteria invented in real time — which means the designer is evaluated against unstated standards and the reviewer's System 1 is doing most of the evaluation.

**Watchout:** Calling every session a "feedback session" to avoid conflict produces no decisions and endless iteration. Calling every session an "approval session" to force closure produces approvals that don't hold because objections weren't surfaced.

## Part 2 — Classifying Feedback in Real Time

**Concept:** Not all feedback is equal, and treating it as equal produces two failures: blocking feedback gets dismissed because it came alongside preferences, and preferences get treated as blockers because they were stated strongly. Three categories cover most of what happens in a review: blocking (must be addressed before the work proceeds and the reviewer has authority to block it), directional (important input the designer should know about but the reviewer is not blocking on it), and informational (context that's useful background but doesn't change direction). The PM's job during a review is to classify feedback as it arrives and reflect the classification back.

The challenge: strong preferences sound like blocking concerns. The test isn't volume or confidence — it's authority and substance. Does this person have the authority to block this feature? Does their concern identify a real problem with the design, or a preference for a different direction?

**Method:**
During a review, for each piece of feedback received:
1. Listen for the substance: is this identifying a failure (the design doesn't accomplish what it needs to accomplish) or expressing a preference (I would have done this differently)?
2. Listen for authority: is this person in a position to block the work? If someone without blocking authority states something as a blocking concern, it's important directional feedback — not a blocker.
3. Reflect the classification back: "I'm hearing this as directional — [designer name] will take this into account in the next revision, but it's not blocking us from moving forward. Does that feel right to you?"
4. Keep a live list in the meeting doc: Blocking / Directional / Informational — assign each feedback item as it comes in

**What you end up with:** A feedback record where every item is classified and every participant knows how their input will be weighted.

**Proof:** When stakeholders see their input classified and reflected back, most directional feedback is accepted as directional. Resistance to the classification is itself a signal: someone resisting being moved from "blocking" to "directional" may have a legitimate concern that hasn't been fully heard, or may be asserting authority they don't have — and the distinction matters for how to respond.

**Watchout:** The classification system can be used to dismiss feedback. If every concern is classified as informational, the system is being misused. The classification should be honest — and if a legitimate blocking concern is misclassified, it will resurface at a worse time.

## Part 3 — Closing on Decisions

**Concept:** A design review that ends with "great, let's think about it" hasn't closed — it's been suspended. The difference between a meeting that produced alignment and a meeting that deferred a decision is the named outcome: "we're going with Option A" or "we're going back to design with two specific changes and returning in one week." A suspension doesn't mean no decision was made — it means no one said what the decision was. When the next meeting opens, no one can agree on what the previous meeting concluded.

Closing on a named decision doesn't require full consensus. It requires clarity: who makes this decision, what's the decision, and when is it effective. In most organizations, design decisions are made by the designer or design lead with PM alignment — not by committee. Named decision-makers prevent the situation where a decision can be reopened by anyone who wasn't fully satisfied.

**Method:**
At the end of every design review, before the session closes:
1. State the outcome explicitly: "Based on today's session, we're [going forward / going back / deferring until X condition is met]"
2. For any "going forward" outcome: name what the design is approved to do and what remains open
3. For any "going back" outcome: name the specific changes that need to be made and the criteria for approval in the next session — this prevents "going back" from becoming an open-ended rework
4. For any "deferring" outcome: name the condition that will resolve the deferral and a target date — a deferral without a resolution condition is a non-decision

**What you end up with:** A named decision at the end of every session that can be referenced, shared, and held to.

**Proof:** Send the decision to participants within 24 hours of the session. If it produces replies of "that's not what I thought we decided," the decision wasn't clear enough during the session. If it produces no replies, it was either clear or people aren't engaged — the next session's opening will clarify which.

**Watchout:** Naming a decision prematurely to end the session before consensus is real. A decision that's named but not actually accepted by blocking parties will be reopened in the next session. The goal is a clear decision, not a fast decision.

## Part 4 — Protecting Decisions Without Dismissing New Input

**Concept:** Approved work gets reopened for two distinct reasons: new information has emerged that genuinely changes the direction (legitimate reason) and a stakeholder who wasn't satisfied with the original decision is re-raising the same objection under new framing (not a legitimate reason for reopening). The PM's job is to distinguish these — because treating both the same way is a mistake in both directions. Dismissing legitimate new information produces the wrong outcome; re-litigating settled work produces iteration that doesn't advance.

The test: does the new input name something that wasn't considered in the original decision? If yes, it may warrant reopening. If the input restates a concern that was raised and resolved — even if the person raising it is different — it should not reopen the decision.

**Method:**
When work that was previously approved is being challenged:
1. Name the original decision explicitly and when it was made: "We approved Option A on [date] based on [criteria]"
2. Ask: "Is there something new here that wasn't available when we made that decision?" — new user evidence, new technical constraint, new business requirement
3. If there's genuine new information: name it specifically, evaluate it against the original criteria, and decide whether it changes the direction — this is productive reopening
4. If there's no new information: acknowledge the concern, reflect it as directional, and hold the original decision: "I hear the concern. We considered that in the original review and decided [outcome]. If there's new information I'm missing, I'd like to understand it — but if this is the same concern from before, I need to hold the decision so we can keep moving."

**What you end up with:** A process for filtering reopening requests that accepts legitimate new input and protects against re-litigation without shutting down conversation.

**Proof:** A PM who can hold a decision under pressure without dismissing the challenger has this working. The tell: the stakeholder understands why the decision is being held even if they disagree with it. If they feel dismissed rather than heard, the protection was too defensive.

**Watchout:** Holding decisions that are genuinely wrong because reopening is uncomfortable. The protection is for decisions that remain correct given what's known. If new information genuinely changes the right answer, the decision should change — and the PM should be the one to name it, not the last to accept it.

**Try This:** In your next design review, before the session starts, write which session type it is (alignment / approval / feedback) and one specific outcome that would close the session successfully. At the end of the session, write whether the outcome was reached and what prevented it if not.

**Take This Further:** Over three consecutive reviews, classify feedback as it arrives (blocking / directional / informational) and share the classification in the meeting. Write one sentence after the third: how did naming the category change what happened to the feedback?

**Judgment Exercise:** You've run a successful approval session. The work is approved. Three days later, a senior stakeholder who was not in the session reviews the design on a shared doc and leaves comments that reopen the approved direction. You have a week before development starts. The stakeholder's comments don't contain new information — they raise the same concerns that were surfaced and resolved in the approval session. What do you do?
