# From a Vague Ask to a Real Persona
**Tier:** 300 — Orchestrate | **Arc:** Full arc (5 parts) | **Prereqs:** 173 (Empathy as Cognitive Simulation), 151 (Self-Report vs. Observed Behavior), 152 (Behavior vs. Attitude), 202 research arc. Complements 125 (JTBD — personas answer "who," JTBD answers "why")
**Note:** Parts 1-2 (Proto-Persona, Role Is Not a Persona) also appear as Tier 100 atoms in the master outline. This file is the single source.

**Goal:** After this arc, you will be able to move from a named role to an honest persona — labeled by confidence level, grounded in what you actually know, and useful in the decisions that follow.

**Trigger:** you're designing for someone whose actual experience you're guessing at — and you want to move from "we think users are like this" to something you can actually stand behind.

**If you don't have research access yet:** Start with **301c (Assumption-First Proto-Persona)** instead of this arc. Build the structured proto-persona from team knowledge, label it honestly, and return to this arc when access opens. The 301c output is designed to feed directly into this arc's Part 3 (From Proto to Real).

## Part 1: Start With Your Proto-Persona

Most personas people write on short notice aren't personas — they're proto-personas: a best guess built from experience, without dedicated research behind it yet. That's not a problem. Pretending otherwise is.

**Method:**
1. Write down what you currently believe about the person you're designing for — their goals, frustrations, what they already know.
2. Label it clearly: "Proto-persona — based on [experience/assumption], not yet validated."
3. Use it as a working starting point, not a finished artifact.

**What you end up with:** a named, honest starting point instead of an unlabeled guess dressed up as research.

**Proof:** A proto-persona labeled as such can be questioned, updated, or replaced as confidence grows. One presented without the label can't be — it's already been treated as settled. The label is what keeps the artifact honest as the work moves forward.

**Watchout:** don't skip the label. Presenting a proto-persona as a finished one is the exact failure this whole arc exists to prevent.

## Part 2: A Role Is Not a Persona

"The approver," "the admin," "the field technician" — these are roles: functions someone performs. A persona is the person behind the function — their context, motivations, and constraints. Confusing the two leads to designs that fit the job title but miss the human doing it.

**Method:**
1. Take a role you're currently designing for.
2. Ask: what does this person actually want, worry about, or get frustrated by — beyond just their job function?
3. If you can't answer that yet, you have a role, not a persona. That's fine — just don't call it one.

**What you end up with:** clarity on whether you're designing for a function or a person — and what's still missing if it's only the former.

**Proof:** The question "what does this person actually want, worry about, or get frustrated by — beyond their job function?" either produces an answer or doesn't. If it doesn't, you have a role, not a persona — and now you know specifically what's missing rather than having a vague feeling the design isn't quite right.

**Watchout:** don't over-engineer this into a new deliverable. It's a lens to apply to what you already have, not an excuse to add process.

## Part 3: From Proto to Real

A persona doesn't flip from "guess" to "real" at some single moment. It moves along a gradient, as your confidence in the underlying data grows.

**Method:** ask, honestly, where did this information come from, and how much do I trust it?
- One offhand comment → still mostly proto.
- A pattern across several real conversations or data points → more real.
- Verified, repeated, cross-checked → as real as it gets.

**What you end up with:** an honest read on how much weight your current persona can actually bear in a decision.

**Proof:** "One offhand comment," "a pattern across several conversations," and "verified, cross-checked" produce different answers to the same design question. Being able to name which level you're at means the weight you're putting on the persona matches the evidence behind it — rather than whatever feels right.

**Watchout:** more data isn't automatically better data. A pile of biased input doesn't make a persona more real — it just makes the bias harder to see.

## Part 4: One Person, Many Roles

The same person might be your approver in the morning and a hands-on user in the afternoon. Personas can quietly assume one fixed identity per person, which isn't how people actually work.

**Method:** before locking in a persona, ask — is this person always in this role, or do they move between several? If they move between roles, decide which one you're actually designing for right now, and say so.

**What you end up with:** a persona that's honest about which slice of a real person it represents, instead of assuming the whole person fits in one box.

**Proof:** "Is this person always in this role, or do they move between several?" is a factual question about the work, not a philosophical one. The answer either adds complexity to the persona or confirms the scope is right. Either way, the design decision that follows it is more grounded than one that ignored the question.

**Watchout:** don't use this as an excuse to avoid ever committing to a primary persona. Multiplicity is real, but you still need to pick a focus to design well.

---

## Part 5: Carrying It Forward

A persona — proto or real — is only useful if it actually gets used in the decisions that follow it.

**Method:**
1. Keep the persona visible somewhere the work actually happens, not buried in a doc nobody reopens.
2. Revisit it when a decision feels uncertain — does this still hold, or has what you know changed?
3. Update the label (proto → more real) as your confidence changes.

**What you end up with:** a living reference instead of a one-time exercise.

**Proof:** A persona revisited at a decision point either still holds or has changed. If it still holds, you've confirmed your assumptions are current. If it's changed, you've caught a drift before it shaped a decision — which is the entire purpose of keeping it visible.

**Watchout:** a stale, unrevisited persona becomes a liability — it starts guiding decisions based on outdated assumptions nobody rechecked. If the persona is the foundation for a significant product direction and primary research is feasible, a Researcher can upgrade the assumption layer with real evidence — the persona stops being a structured guess and becomes something that can withstand scrutiny.

**Try This:** take whatever persona or user description you're currently working from. Write two things: (1) the confidence label it deserves right now ("proto-persona — based on [what]"), and (2) the one question, if answered, that would move it one level closer to real.

**Take this further:** in the next two weeks, revisit the persona you labeled in Try This. Has your confidence level changed based on anything you've observed or heard since? Write one sentence: what would it take to move it one more level toward real?

**Judgment exercise:** You're designing for users you can't reach — they're employees of a client company and you're not allowed to contact them directly. The arc's core mechanism is raising confidence by checking assumptions against real people. What parts of the arc still apply? What do you use instead of access? What do you give up?

**After you've run this yourself:** paste your current proto-persona or user description into an AI tool and ask: "What assumptions in this persona am I taking for granted and would need to validate to raise my confidence?" — treat the output as a checklist of what to test, not a critique.
