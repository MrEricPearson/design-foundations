# Manipulative/Dark-Pattern Awareness (practitioner-level)
**Tier:** 300 — Orchestrate | **Arc:** Standalone | **Prereqs:** None. Pairs with 115 (giving and receiving feedback) for how to name this in a review, and 136 (critique vs. feedback) for the mode to use when raising it.

**Goal:** After this piece, you will be able to name whether a persuasive design choice works because it helps the person or because it exploits a decision shortcut — and know the difference before it ships.

**Trigger:** a design choice is technically effective at getting people to do something, and it's worth pausing to check why it works.

**Method:** ask honestly — does this work because it's genuinely helpful, or because it exploits a shortcut in how people make decisions (urgency, guilt, confusion)? If it's the latter, that's worth reconsidering even if it "performs."

**Artifact:** one honest gut-check on a persuasive design choice already in use or under consideration.

**Proof:** "Does this work because it's helpful or because it exploits a decision shortcut?" is answerable for any specific design choice. A technique that can only be justified by its performance metric — not by what it does for the person — has already answered the question.

**Watchout:** this isn't about policing every persuasive technique — plenty of legitimate design creates urgency or simplicity. The line is whether it helps the person or just extracts a decision from them.

**When you're not the decision-maker:** If the pattern is live and you don't have authority to change it, the method still has a use — it gives you a named, specific framing for raising the concern rather than a vague objection. "This creates urgency by implying scarcity that doesn't exist" is a specific claim; "this feels manipulative" is a feeling. A specific claim is more actionable for whoever does have authority to respond to it. Name what the pattern is doing and what decision shortcut it exploits — then raise it to the person who can act on it. What you can control: the quality and specificity of the concern you raise. What you may not be able to control: whether it changes.

**Try This:** find one persuasive element in something you're currently building or reviewing — a call to action, a notification, a default setting, a countdown. Ask: could you defend this choice by saying "this makes it easier for the person to get what they actually want" — or only by saying "it increases conversion"? If only the second, that's the answer.

**Take this further:** in the next week, run the same gut-check on one persuasive element in a product you use but didn't build. Write one sentence: does the line feel clearer or more ambiguous when you're evaluating someone else's design instead of your own?

**Judgment exercise:** The pattern is already live and performing well — leadership is citing conversion numbers. You're not the decision-maker. The method gives you a way to identify a dark pattern; it doesn't give you organizational authority to stop it. What can you do with this arc from where you actually stand? What can you say, to whom, and with what framing — and what do you accept you may not be able to change?

**What Next:** if a colleague is using a pattern you want to flag without making it confrontational, read 136 (Critique vs. Feedback) to set the mode before the conversation. If the pattern is systemic across a product rather than one isolated choice, it's a 304 (Workshop) conversation about shared standards — not a one-to-one design critique.
