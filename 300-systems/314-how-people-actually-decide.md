# How People Actually Decide
**Tier:** 300 — Orchestrate | **Arc:** Full arc (4 parts) | **Audience:** Cross-audience | **Prereqs:** 247 (Dual Process Theory), 248 (Framing Effect), 249 (Loss Aversion), 250 (Status Quo Bias), 252 (Anchoring), 256 (Hick's Law), 260 (Decision Fatigue)

**Goal:** Identify which cognitive mechanism is shaping a decision that keeps getting deferred, reversed, or made differently than expected — and design the decision context, not just the options, to support the quality of thinking the decision deserves.

**Trigger:** A decision keeps getting deferred, reopened, or made in ways that don't track with the quality of the options presented. You've made the rational case repeatedly and the outcome still isn't matching the logic. The problem may be the decision context, not the decision.

---

## Part 1 — The Two-System Problem in Decision Meetings

**Concept:** Most decisions in product and organizational contexts are made by System 1, not System 2 — even when the people involved believe they're deliberating (247). S1 is faster than deliberation and almost always presents its conclusions to S2 as pre-formed opinions. S2 then often rationalizes those conclusions rather than evaluating them independently. Designing a decision context only for S2 — providing data, logic, structured arguments — assumes S2 is already engaged before S1 has produced a verdict. In most meetings, that assumption is wrong.

The question to ask before designing any decision context is: what will S1 already think about this before any deliberation starts? What patterns will S1 match it to? What will feel like a threat, a risk, or an imposition before anyone has read the supporting material? A decision context designed only for S2 will fail when S1 has already produced a verdict and S2 is performing rather than evaluating.

**Method:**
Before bringing any significant decision to a group:
1. Write the decision question as neutrally as possible — what is actually being decided?
2. Predict the S1 verdict: what will most people in this room feel about this option before deliberation begins? Is there a threat signal (something being taken away, something new being imposed, an investment being declared wrong)? Name it.
3. If a strong S1 verdict is likely, ask: does the decision context I'm designing open S2 engagement or close it? Does the information arrive before or after S1 has already decided?
4. Design the entry: how does information arrive before the S1 verdict fires? Can you name the question before naming the options? Can you surface evaluation criteria before revealing the recommendation?

**What you end up with:** A decision framing that creates conditions for S2 engagement before S1 polarizes the room.

**Proof:** A room where S1 has pre-decided argues about the recommendation ("I think we should do X instead"). A room where S2 engaged first argues about which option best satisfies the criteria. The latter conversation is slower, more effortful, and more likely to produce a durable decision.

**Watchout:** "Helping S1 not pre-decide" is not manipulation — it's removing the conditions that prevent genuine deliberation. The distinction is whether you're opening cognitive space (naming criteria before options, naming the question before the recommendation) or closing it (timing information to prevent evaluation of alternatives you've already rejected).

## Part 2 — Anchoring and Framing the Decision Space

**Concept:** The first option, number, or frame introduced in a decision context becomes the reference point S1 uses to evaluate everything that follows — regardless of whether the first element was intended as a reference point (252, 248). In a planning meeting, the first estimate mentioned is the gravity field for all subsequent estimates. In a design review, the first option shown is the implicit baseline. In a stakeholder presentation, "our recommendation is X" anchors the room to defending or attacking X before alternatives are considered. This happens even when the first element is explicitly labeled as preliminary.

The additional mechanism: S1 treats the current state — whatever exists today — as the baseline against which everything else is measured. The status quo is not one option among several; it is the zero point (250). Any option presented without also naming the status quo as an explicit alternative becomes a comparison to an invisible, zero-cost baseline that's already in S1's favor.

**Method:**
Before any decision presentation:
1. State the decision criteria explicitly before showing any options — what does a good decision need to satisfy?
2. Name the status quo as an explicit option, including its costs — what does it cost to stay as we are? (this is required because S1 treats the status quo as free; naming its costs is the only way to put both options on equal cognitive footing)
3. Present options in parallel, not sequentially, to the extent possible — a table or structured comparison reduces the anchoring advantage of the first-presented option
4. If numbers are involved, present a range rather than a single estimate; if a recommendation exists, present it last, after the room has engaged with the criteria and options

**What you end up with:** A decision space where the first frame is the decision question and criteria, not the preferred answer — which creates the structural conditions for the room to engage evaluatively.

**Proof:** A room that has seen the status quo named as an explicit option makes a different decision than a room where the status quo was invisible. The naming forces S1 to evaluate it rather than treat it as the costless baseline — which is the minimum required for a genuine decision to occur.

**Watchout:** Naming options strategically to guide a decision toward your preferred outcome is different from naming options fairly. The goal of this method is removing accidental anchoring, not installing deliberate anchoring. A decision context that names the status quo as an option but describes it in terms designed to make it look bad is still anchoring — just in the other direction.

## Part 3 — The Effort Cost of Deciding

**Concept:** Decision-making is cognitively expensive, and S2 resources deplete across a session (260). More options extend decision time exponentially rather than linearly — halving options nearly eliminates decision time, not just reduces it (256). Together, these two mechanisms mean that important decisions made at the end of a long meeting, or decisions that follow too many other decisions, are made by a depleted S2 under greater S1 influence. The room that made thoughtful early decisions is not the same room making late ones — not because the people changed, but because the resource they used for deliberation has been spent.

Post-depletion decisions have a predictable pattern: they default either toward the most salient option (impulsive resolution) or toward the status quo (conservative avoidance of additional cost). Both can look like agreement. Neither is deliberation.

**Method:**
For any decision meeting:
1. Place the most important decision first — not last after the easier, less contentious items have been cleared; consequential decisions deserve full S2 capacity
2. Limit the number of options presented at any decision point to three or fewer; if more exist, narrow to three before the meeting rather than in it
3. Pre-work the decisions that require the most options or background: send material before the meeting so processing cost has already been paid before the room convenes
4. Observe decision quality across the session: when the room becomes either unusually agreeable or unusually resistant, that is a depletion signal — not evidence the decision is settled or unsettleable

**What you end up with:** A meeting structure that allocates cognitive resources to the decisions that most need them.

**Proof:** Agenda order is a decision variable. A group that would have made a careful decision at 9am makes a different decision at 4pm, not because of new information but because of resource depletion. If you suspect a bad decision was made late in a session, reopen it at the start of the next one and observe whether the outcome changes.

**Watchout:** Reducing options is not making the decision for the room — it's reducing the cognitive cost of engaging with the decision. The pre-work of narrowing is editorial, not political: the goal is presenting the real decision space without the cognitive overhead of options the room would eliminate anyway.

## Part 4 — Why Loss Looms Larger Than Gain

**Concept:** Any proposal that involves removing, changing, or sunsetting something will face stronger resistance than an equivalent proposal for adding something new — regardless of the objective improvement involved (249, 250). This is structural, not personal: losses register approximately twice as strongly as equivalent gains in S1 processing, and the current state is treated as a free baseline that any change must overcome. A proposal to add a feature and a proposal to replace a feature with a better version are not equivalent psychologically, even if the outcome is identical.

The practical implication: presentations that lead with what's lost produce more resistance than presentations that lead with what's preserved. This is not spin — both descriptions can be equally accurate. The choice of which accurate description to lead with is a design decision about which S1 reaction to invoke first.

**Method:**
For any proposal involving removal, significant change, or transition:
1. Explicitly name what's preserved before naming what changes — write this out, don't assume it's obvious
2. Name the cost of the current state in terms the audience cares about, before describing the change: time lost, errors created, workarounds required, things currently impossible that will become possible; this is required because Loss Aversion activates on both directions — if the current state has visible costs, those costs can activate the same mechanism against inaction
3. If a feature or practice is being sunset, provide a specific transition path before the cutoff date, not after; uncertainty about what comes next amplifies Loss Aversion
4. If usage data is available, present it before describing the change — data grounds the decision in the current state's actual performance rather than its imagined value

**What you end up with:** A proposal structure that activates Loss Aversion symmetrically — both the cost of change and the cost of inaction are visible — rather than asymmetrically in favor of inaction.

**Proof:** "We're removing X and replacing it with Y, which is better" produces more resistance than "Y does everything X does plus Z, so we're able to consolidate both into one place" — even when both sentences describe the identical change. The framing is not spin; it's the difference between a loss frame and a preservation frame, applied to the same accurate description.

**Watchout:** If the change genuinely is a loss with limited gain, leading with preservation framing fails honestly — there's nothing to name as preserved. In that case, address the genuine loss directly and describe explicitly what is being done to mitigate it. This technique fails and damages trust when it's used to obscure rather than clarify.

**Judgment Exercise:** You're designing an onboarding flow for a new tool. Including personalization choices at setup increases user investment and ownership — the IKEA Effect means users who configure something feel more responsible for it working. Including fewer choices reduces drop-off — Hick's Law and Decision Fatigue mean more choices produce more abandonment. The two mechanisms point in opposite directions. You can only test one version before launch. Which version do you test? What specific signal from the test would change your answer about which version to ship?
