# Running One Workshop, Start to Finish
**Tier:** 300 — Orchestrate | **Arc:** Full arc (5 parts) | **Prereqs:** 115 (giving and receiving feedback), 163 (Alignment vs. Consensus), 164 (Generative vs. Convergent Thinking); deploys 207 (premortem), 203 (Crazy 8s), 206 journey mapping arc as toolkit options. Also pairs with 129 (Stakeholder Mapping) for identifying who needs to be in the room.

**Goal:** After this arc, you will be able to decide whether a workshop is warranted, shape it for a specific outcome, run it without losing the room, capture committed decisions, and verify follow-through weeks later.

**Trigger:** an alignment problem has resisted smaller conversations and needs a structured session to surface what the room actually thinks.

---

## Part 1: Is It Even Needed — and If So, For What

Not every alignment problem needs a workshop. Sometimes a shorter conversation does the same job. And when a workshop is the right call, knowing what you're trying to accomplish shapes what goes in it.

**Method:**
1. Ask whether the issue is that people don't understand each other's perspectives, or just that a decision hasn't been made yet. The first often needs a workshop. The second usually just needs someone to decide.
2. If a workshop is the right call, name the goal before designing the session. Three common goals, each with a different toolkit:
   - **Surfacing risks before committing** → reach for Premortem (207)
   - **Generating options the group hasn't considered** → reach for Structured Ideation / Crazy 8s (203)
   - **Building a shared picture of an existing experience** → reach for Journey Mapping (206)
3. Pick one. A workshop with no defined technique will fill with discussion; one with a technique will produce something.

**What you end up with:** a clear reason for holding (or skipping) the workshop, and — if holding — a specific technique selected for what you're trying to accomplish.

**Proof:** A workshop with a stated goal and a selected technique produces a tangible output: a failure-mode list, a set of rough options, or a current-state map. One without a technique produces a summary of what was discussed — which is harder to act on.

**Watchout:** "no workshop needed" can become a convenient excuse to skip alignment entirely. Make sure the decision is genuine, not avoidance. And choosing a technique doesn't mean rigidly following it — adjust based on what the room needs.

---

## Part 2: Opening Safely

How a session opens sets whether people will actually say what they think, or just go along with the room.

**Method:** start by naming, briefly and genuinely, that disagreement is welcome and useful in this session — not as a formality, but meant. Ask an easy, low-stakes question first to get people talking before the harder ones come up.

**What you end up with:** a room slightly more willing to say what they actually think.

**Proof:** A low-stakes opening question that gets people talking before the harder ones arrive is structurally different from going straight to the hard question. The room's first response sets the temperature for everything that follows — starting with something easy changes what the room thinks is possible to say.

**Watchout:** don't let this feel forced or HR-flavored. Keep it natural and brief, not a scripted ritual.

---

## Part 3: Handling a Skeptic

A skeptical voice in the room is often naming a real risk, even if it feels like friction in the moment.

**Method:** when someone pushes back, ask them to say more before responding — "what's the concern, specifically?" Often the real issue underneath is more useful than the initial objection sounded.

**What you end up with:** either a real risk surfaced and addressed, or a resolved misunderstanding — both are better than the skepticism staying unspoken.

**Proof:** Asking "what's the concern, specifically?" either produces a specific concern worth addressing or it doesn't. If it does, you've surfaced something real. If it resolves in a clarification, you've resolved it faster than leaving it unaddressed. Either way, the question costs less than the alternative.

**Watchout:** don't shut them down to keep things smooth. They're often naming something worth hearing.

---

## Part 4: Closing the Session

A session that ends without clear next steps loses most of its value within a day.

**Method:** before ending, state out loud what was decided, who owns what, and by when. Write it down somewhere visible immediately — don't rely on memory.

**What you end up with:** commitments that are far more likely to actually happen.

**Proof:** A written commitment with a name and a date can be checked. A general sense of agreement after a session can't. The writing-down at the end isn't a formality — it's what distinguishes a commitment from a feeling.

**Watchout:** a rushed close loses commitments people forget by tomorrow. Don't let this be the part that gets cut for time.

---

## Part 5: Following Up Weeks Later

The real test of a workshop isn't the session itself — it's whether anything actually changed afterward.

**Method:** a few weeks later, revisit what was decided. Did it happen? If not, why not, and is that worth addressing?

**What you end up with:** an honest read on whether the workshop actually worked, not just whether it felt productive at the time.

**Proof:** "Did the commitments from Part 4 actually happen?" is checkable. A follow-up that produces a yes is confirmation the session worked. A follow-up that produces a no — or a "mostly, but..." — is information about what didn't hold and why, which is more valuable than never knowing.

**Watchout:** this is the step most likely to quietly get skipped. Put a reminder on it before you forget.

## When You Can't Run the Full Version

**Remote-First Workshops**

The arc assumes participants in one room with shared physical (or live digital) presence. Remote-first workshops — distributed time zones, camera-off norms, participants joining late — require adjusted mechanics for each part.

**Part 1 (Is It Needed):** No change. The question of whether a session is warranted is the same regardless of format.

**Part 2 (Opening Safely):** In remote contexts, a low-stakes opening question works better in writing first before voice. Put it in the chat before asking verbally: "Before we open audio, drop one word in the chat: what would a useful outcome of this session look like?" Reading written responses before the verbal discussion changes the room's opening temperature — participants who haven't yet spoken have still contributed.

**Part 3 (Handling a Skeptic):** No change. "What's the concern, specifically?" works the same remotely. The risk in remote is that the skeptic stays silent rather than speaking. Monitor the chat for pushback that isn't being voiced.

**Part 4 (Closing):** Remote sessions require the written record more urgently than in-person ones — people leave a call and immediately lose shared context. Write commitments to a visible shared document during the session (not after), read them back before ending, and share the document in the session channel before the call ends. Don't rely on a follow-up email.

**Part 5 (Following Up):** No change. Put the follow-up on your calendar before you end the session.

**What you still get:** Alignment, surfaced risks, and captured commitments — the core outcomes of the arc.

**What you give up:** The physical and social dynamics that make generative moments spark differently in a room. The serendipitous connections between people that happen before and after the session. The body-language reading that tells a facilitator when the room has shifted.

**Don't do this:** Don't attempt a generative session (Crazy 8s, journey mapping) with participants who are camera-off, distributed, and joining from time zones 8+ hours apart. The synchronous generative work requires enough shared cognitive presence to make the mode possible. Use async pre-work to generate, and a shorter synchronous session only to converge and commit.

---

**Large-Group Facilitation (15+ participants)**

The arc's facilitation moves — one opening question, direct skeptic handling, visible commitment-writing — work for rooms up to approximately 12 people. Above 15, the mechanics must change.

**Structure the session around breakouts.** Never run generative work with a group of 15+ in plenary. Divide into breakout groups of 4-6, run the generative phase in breakouts, and use the plenary session for reporting back and converging. If you can't run breakouts (synchronous remote with no breakout rooms), run the generative phase as async pre-work before the plenary session.

**Convergence in large groups.** Dot voting, straw polls, or silent individual ranking before group discussion — these prevent the loudest voice in the room from setting the convergence. Write the options being voted on where everyone can see them simultaneously before any vote is cast.

**Commitment capture in large groups.** Commitments in large groups are owned by named individuals, not by "the group." Each commitment exits the session with one person's name on it. A group commitment is not actionable; an individual commitment is.

---

**Try This:** think of an alignment problem you're currently sitting with. Run the Part 1 check right now: what specific decision needs to come out of a session with this group? If you can't name it, a workshop isn't what's needed yet — a conversation to clarify the question is.

**Take this further:** in the next two weeks, follow up on whatever was decided in the workshop. Write one sentence: what held, and what quietly got reinterpreted between the session and now?

**Judgment exercise:** You've planned the workshop and the key decision-maker isn't coming — they're sending a delegate who can't commit on their behalf. The arc assumes the people in the room can make the decisions the workshop is designed to produce. What do you adapt? What outcome do you give up? Is the session still worth running, and what does it become if the answer is yes?

**What Next:** if the workshop surfaces a shared problem but no clear direction, 203 (Crazy 8s) is the ideation method to run next. If it surfaces a committed direction that needs to survive a handoff, read 303 (One Feature, Three Handoffs) before the commitments get silently reinterpreted.
