# Solution-First Rapid Ideation
**Tier:** 300 — Orchestrate | **Arc:** Full arc (4 parts) | **Audience:** Cross-audience (all practitioners) | **Prereqs:** 100 (Assumption vs. Fact), 101 (Not All Assumptions Are Equal), 103 (Attachment), 113 (Defining Success Before You Start), 147 (AI as Execution Partner), 247 (Dual Process Theory), 249 (Loss Aversion)

**Goal:** Use creation as a discovery tool — by writing an explicit hypothesis before building, using the built artifact to surface what you didn't know to ask, running a structured learning loop within 48 hours, and knowing the three conditions under which the flipped process is appropriate.

**Trigger:** The cost of creating a first version is near zero. You could write a document, build a prototype, generate a draft, or produce a working artifact in the time it would take to frame the problem. Or: discovery research is unclear or unavailable, and you have enough domain knowledge to take a productive first swing. Or: you've been in discovery for too long and have diminishing returns on more research — creation would generate more signal than another conversation. The traditional discovery-before-creation sequence is not required — but flipping it carries risks that the standard sequence avoids.

---

## Part 1 — Why the Sequence Can Flip

**Concept:** The standard design sequence — discover the problem, then create a solution — exists for a reason: creation is expensive, and building the wrong thing has high reversal cost. When creation cost was high (weeks of development, significant design investment), doing discovery first was economically rational. When creation cost approaches zero — because AI can draft a first version, because a prototype can be built in hours, because a test can be written before the feature — the economic rationale for sequencing discovery first weakens.

What doesn't weaken: the risk of attachment. When you invest in creating something, you form an emotional stake in it. The IKEA Effect means effort produces investment. Loss Aversion means the prospect of abandoning what you built feels costly. Attachment is not proportional to cost — a two-hour prototype can produce as much attachment as a two-week build, because attachment tracks effort and authorship, not hours or dollars.

The flipped sequence is: create → learn from the creation → discover what you didn't know to ask. Discovery is the result, not the prerequisite. The creation is not the product — it's the instrument for generating questions that research couldn't generate. The condition for this to work: the creator must hold the created artifact lightly enough to abandon or transform it completely when the learning arrives.

**Method:**
Before flipping the sequence, run three gate checks:
1. **Hypothesis gate:** can you write a specific, testable hypothesis before starting? "I believe that [user type] experiencing [situation] will find [this approach] useful because [reasoning]." If you can't write it, you don't have enough of a frame for creation to generate useful learning — you're producing output, not testing a hypothesis
2. **Reversal cost gate:** what's the cost of abandoning or fundamentally changing what you create? The flipped sequence is appropriate for Low-to-Medium reversal cost only. High reversal cost (platform-level decisions, significant stakeholder commitments, major development investment) requires discovery first
3. **Exposure control gate:** can you control who sees the artifact and when? The learning loop requires that the created artifact is used for learning before it's treated as a deliverable. If stakeholders will see it as a commitment before you've run the learning loop, the process has failed before the loop can run

**What you end up with:** A gate check that determines whether the flipped sequence is appropriate for this specific creation — not a blanket permission or prohibition.

**Proof:** If you can write the hypothesis, the reversal cost is manageable, and you control exposure — the sequence can flip. If any of the three gates fails, the standard sequence is lower risk.

**Watchout:** The hypothesis gate is the most important. "I'll figure out what I'm testing after I see what I make" is not a hypothesis — it's creating without an intent to learn. The flipped sequence only works when the creation is testing something specific.

## Part 2 — Creating With Learning Intent

**Concept:** Creating with learning intent is different from creating with quality intent. When quality is the goal, the creator optimizes for the best possible first version — cleaning, refining, removing rough edges. When learning is the goal, the creator optimizes for the artifact that will generate the most useful signal — which is often messier, more explicit about its assumptions, and structured to make failure visible rather than obscured.

A creation built for learning should make its reasoning transparent: what did you assume here? Where are the places where a different decision would produce a different outcome? What would someone looking at this misunderstand, and is that misunderstanding the question you're testing? Creating with learning intent means building the artifact to be interrogated, not admired.

**Method:**
While creating with learning intent:
1. **Mark the assumptions:** as you create, annotate or note the decisions you're making that rest on assumptions about users, context, or behavior — not to slow down creation, but to make the assumption surface during the learning loop
2. **Make the critical assumption explicit:** identify the one assumption that, if wrong, would make the whole artifact wrong — this is the primary test for the learning loop
3. **Build to the hypothesis, not to polish:** if your hypothesis is about whether users can find a feature, build just enough to test wayfinding — don't polish what's beyond the wayfinding decision
4. **Set a time constraint:** the creation phase in a solution-first process should be time-boxed — enough time to produce something testable, not enough time to produce something you'd be reluctant to change; 2-4 hours for most artifacts is the right window; more than a day suggests either the hypothesis is too large or the reversal cost is higher than assessed

**What you end up with:** A created artifact that's ready for the learning loop — assumption-visible, hypothesis-testable, and held lightly enough to change.

**Proof:** If the creation took significantly longer than the time constraint, check whether attachment is forming. If you're reluctant to share it yet because it's "not ready," that's polish-seeking in a learning context — and a signal that the intent has shifted from learning to quality. That shift is when the flipped sequence starts to fail.

**Watchout:** AI-assisted creation accelerates the production of artifacts but doesn't reduce attachment proportionally. The IKEA Effect applies to AI-assisted creation differently but still operates: the act of prompting, refining, and directing even an AI-generated artifact produces ownership. The learning intent discipline is still required.

## Part 3 — The Learning Loop

**Concept:** The learning loop is what makes solution-first creation different from just starting. Without a structured learning loop, creation-first becomes: build a thing, show it to people, incorporate feedback, iterate on what you built. That's not discovery — it's just development with early feedback. The learning loop specifically asks: what did creating this reveal that you didn't know to ask before? What did you learn about the problem space by attempting a solution?

The questions the learning loop is designed to answer are different from the questions a standard review produces. A review asks "is this good?" The learning loop asks "what does this tell us about the problem?" Those are different evaluations of the same artifact, and only one of them serves the purpose of solution-first ideation.

**Method:**
Within 48 hours of completing the creation:
1. **Review against the hypothesis:** was the hypothesis testable from the artifact? What did using or reviewing the artifact tell you about whether the hypothesis was correct?
2. **Identify the surprises:** what did creating this reveal that you didn't predict? These are the most valuable outputs of the process — the things that research could have taken weeks to surface and creation exposed immediately
3. **Map what changed:** what do you now know about the problem that you didn't know before you created this? How does that knowledge change the direction?
4. **Make the explicit decision:** given the learning, what's the next step? Iterate (the artifact is roughly right, refine it), pivot (the artifact surfaced a better direction, change course), or abandon (the artifact revealed the hypothesis was wrong, go back to problem definition)
5. **Communicate the decision and its basis:** tell the people who need to know what you learned and what you decided — not just what you built

**What you end up with:** A completed learning loop that converts a created artifact into discovery — knowledge about the problem space that the artifact generated.

**Proof:** If the learning loop review produces "yes, the artifact is good, let's continue refining it" as the primary output, the loop wasn't designed for learning — it was designed for feedback. The learning loop should produce at least one surprise: something the creation revealed that wasn't in the original hypothesis.

**Watchout:** The 48-hour constraint on the learning loop is not arbitrary. Delay between creation and learning loop allows attachment to increase (the creator lives with the artifact, invests more time in it, shows it to more people). Longer delays make it progressively harder to abandon or transform the artifact even when learning demands it.

## Part 4 — When This Process Fails

**Concept:** Solution-first rapid ideation fails in predictable ways, and each failure mode is driven by a mechanism that the process is designed to prevent. Knowing the failure modes in advance allows practitioners to monitor for them rather than discovering them after significant investment.

The three primary failure modes: creation without a hypothesis produces output rather than learning; attachment overrides the learning loop (the creator holds the artifact too tightly to update it when learning demands change); and exposure happens before the learning loop (stakeholders treat a learning artifact as a commitment, making it impossible to abandon without a social cost that wasn't priced into the gate check).

Each failure mode produces a specific pattern, and each has a specific recovery.

**Method:**
Monitor for these failure modes during the process:
1. **Creation without a hypothesis:** signal is that the created artifact doesn't clearly test anything — it's a reasonable thing to build, but you couldn't write a "learning that would cause me to change direction" statement. Recovery: stop creating and write the hypothesis before continuing. If you can't write it, return to problem definition.
2. **Attachment overriding learning:** signal is reluctance to show the artifact to the people who would give the most challenging feedback; finding reasons the learning loop findings don't apply; emphasis on iteration over transformation. Recovery: name it explicitly — "I'm attached to this." Then share it with one person who doesn't know the context and has no stake in the hypothesis, and ask them what problem they think it solves.
3. **Premature exposure:** signal is that the artifact is being discussed in language that treats it as a decision rather than a hypothesis — "we're going to build it this way" rather than "we're testing whether this direction is right." Recovery: clarify the artifact's status explicitly with the people who have seen it: "this is a learning artifact, not a commitment; the learning loop hasn't run yet and the direction may change."

**What you end up with:** A monitoring practice that catches the failure modes before they produce wasted effort or sunk cost.

**Proof:** The process is working when the learning loop produces genuine change at least some of the time — when the artifact is transformed or abandoned, not always iterated. A solution-first process that always results in refining the first artifact rather than ever pivoting or abandoning it isn't generating learning — it's generating validation.

**Watchout:** The failure mode of premature exposure is the hardest to recover from in practice. Once an artifact has been seen by stakeholders as a commitment, the social cost of abandoning it exceeds the technical cost of continuing with it. This is why the exposure control gate (Part 1) is critical — the process requires controlling who sees the artifact and in what framing until the learning loop has run.

**Try This:** Take a decision you're currently trying to make that has a clear enough frame to take a first swing. Apply the three gate checks from Part 1. If you pass all three, create an artifact (document, prototype, draft, sketch) with a time constraint of 2 hours. Write the hypothesis before you start. Run the learning loop within 48 hours. What did creating it reveal that research hadn't?

**Take This Further:** Over three solution-first cycles, track what the learning loop produces. Write one sentence after each: what surprised you, and what would you have had to do to learn that through traditional discovery? After the third: when does solution-first generate learning faster than discovery-first, and when doesn't it?

**Judgment Exercise:** You've been creating solution-first for two days — longer than the time constraint. The artifact is sophisticated. You've shown it to two stakeholders who responded positively. The learning loop hasn't formally run yet. You're about to enter a planning meeting where the artifact will likely be discussed as a direction. Recognizing that you may be in the attachment failure mode and approaching premature exposure — what do you do right now, before the meeting?
